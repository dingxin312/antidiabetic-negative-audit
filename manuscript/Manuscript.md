# Negative labels in antidiabetic-peptide prediction: Benchmark reuse and implications for food-peptide screening

## Abstract

**Background:** Peptide predictors help select candidates for synthesis or testing from food-derived libraries, but the meaning of a negative training label varies. A zero may denote measured non-response, missing activity annotation or a comparator assembled by a sampling rule. The resulting benchmark can test a different distinction from the one faced during candidate selection.

**Scope and approach:** This critical review examines negative-label definitions and released sequence versions in 21 purposively selected DPP-IV and antidiabetic-peptide predictors. Sequence-level comparisons and two accessible implementations are considered alongside published experiments that report candidate selection and peptide assays. The focus is what benchmark results establish about screening a specified library.

**Key findings and conclusions:** Negative sequences were retrievable for 17 studies. Seven versions share 665 negatives and their 532/133 training/test membership; seven identities have opposing labels, and two have published DPP-IV inhibition assays. Earlier test sequences also appear in later development or external-test files; in two inspected implementations, test outcomes inform particular model-selection decisions. In a fully assayed library of 22 designed pentapeptides, all eight predicted negatives inhibited DPP-IV, including the most potent peptide. Other food-protein studies assayed shortlists formed after several filters, leaving the activity of discarded candidates unknown. These findings support evaluating a screening rule against measured peptides from both its preferred and rejected groups within a defined library. They characterise the examined versions and selection studies, without estimating performance across the field.

**Keywords:** food-derived peptides; antidiabetic activity; negative samples; benchmark provenance; candidate selection; model evaluation

# 1. Introduction

Food-peptide screening turns a list of possible sequences into a much smaller set for experimental testing. The starting list may consist of peptides identified in a hydrolysate, fragments predicted from a food protein or sequences designed for synthesis. BERT-DPPIV guided the synthesis of DPP-IV inhibitory peptides, while a study of duckweed hydrolysates used several criteria to select measured peptides for synthesis and assays (Guan et al., 2023; Cournoyer et al., 2025). Other studies began with hundreds or thousands of candidates and assayed only a few after computational filtering (Zhang et al., 2024; Bao et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025). Reviews have described the range of available discovery workflows (Chang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). For a researcher choosing which peptides to test, however, the important quantity is not just how many selected peptides prove active. It is also how many active peptides the selection rule leaves behind in the same starting library.

That selection decision depends partly on what the predictor was trained to call negative. The same zero can represent an assayed peptide below a specified response threshold, a sequence with no recorded target activity, an antiviral peptide used for contrast or a randomly generated sequence. AntiDMPpred uses antiviral peptides for which antidiabetic activity was not reported; ADP-Fuse combines randomly selected and other-function sequences (Chen et al., 2022; Basith et al., 2023; Qureshi et al., 2014). Missing annotation differs from observed inactivity, as formalised in positive-unlabelled learning (Elkan and Noto, 2008). Constructed comparators may nevertheless be useful: adding untested molecule-target pairs improved one screening benchmark but reduced performance in a temporal evaluation (Cáceres et al., 2020). A reported score therefore needs to be read against the particular negative group used to produce it.

Current reviews cover food-peptide discovery workflows, assay conditions and approaches to model choice (Chang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2027). Negative-set experiments in antimicrobial-peptide prediction have shown that sampling rules can change a benchmark result (Sidorczuk et al., 2022). The ADP-Hybrid preprint examines homology-aware evaluation in one antidiabetic benchmark and fragment-label patterns in 16 other peptide datasets (Islam et al., 2026, preprint). Less attention has been paid to whether published antidiabetic predictors share negative sequences, labels and assignments to training or test sets, and to how those reference results relate to selecting new peptides. The released versions provide a way to examine these questions at the sequence level.

The distinction between a training label and an evaluation decision is already central to DOME and REFORMS (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024). Here, three related observations are examined separately: the evidence behind an assigned negative label, the population and decisions behind a benchmark score, and measured activity across a library from which candidates are selected (Figure 1). The first two can be investigated from released datasets and code. The third requires assays of peptides from the intended candidate population, including some that the predictor would rank poorly. An experimental result confirms the activity of the peptide tested, regardless of how it was produced; it does not by itself measure the yield or coverage of the rule used to select it.

# 2. Review scope and evidence approach

This review combines a focused literature synthesis with a bounded audit of released predictor data. Literature was assembled from an existing food-peptide and modelling collection and citation tracing; sources were included when they informed negative-label meaning, dataset construction, evaluation or peptide selection. No systematic search or field-wide sampling frame was used. The predictor sample and accessible sequence versions were fixed on 2 November 2026. Milk-protein references were frozen on 5 November, and the candidate-study tables were cross-checked by 7 November. Papers assigned later issue years were included only if already available online; preprints are identified in the text. Supplementary Table S1 records the literature evidence used for the synthesis.

The predictor audit comprises 20 previously identified sequence-based DPP-IV or antidiabetic-peptide studies (A1–A14 and H1–H6), with Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1) added after the sample was fixed to examine its reported re-curation and external panel. All 21 remain in the denominator, including records without retrievable negative sequences (Supplementary Tables S2–S3). The studies address different targets, including general antidiabetic activity, DPP-IV inhibition, diabetes-type categories and residue-level prediction; those outputs were not treated as interchangeable (Basith et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024; Basith et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026, preprint). Comparisons use the available version of each dataset, including an archived A3 copy, files deposited for A5, the H6 model input and a pinned B1 repository version. Published food-peptide studies were selected separately when their starting library, filtering steps or assayed group could be identified. They illustrate peptide-selection comparisons and are not added to the 21-study audit; A5 reports the assayed design library, which A4 later evaluated retrospectively.

Each accessible version was assessed for negative-set completeness, relationship to a sequence reference set and split identity (Table 2; Supplementary Table S3). “Complete” means that all negative members of the stated version were obtained; “partial” includes incomplete deposits and unresolved links between the paper and available files. Source-linked or inaccessible versions and noncomparable tasks were marked “not verified.” Relationships to F665 or F966 record exact membership, containment, length filtering or re-curation; the names refer to sequence sets, not assumed historical pathways. Negative-set membership in the training and test files was checked sequence by sequence against H1, while own splits and development pools were recorded separately. For example, all 629 H6 input negatives were obtained, although they form a length-filtered F665 subset with a new random split (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026). A9 provides 899 development negatives in a pool of 1,798 positive and negative records, but the members of its reported internal 80/20 division were not recovered (Xie et al., 2025). A6 remains linked by source to A2, with no independently verified deposit (Arshad et al., 2024).

Original sequences and source locators were retained alongside normalised strings. Normalisation removed whitespace and standardised case; terminal `X` padding was removed only for A7, whose authors documented that encoding. Records, unique sequence identities, opposite labels and training and test set intersections were counted separately. Published assays were matched to two conflicting identities by exact peptide sequence; the assay search was targeted rather than exhaustive (Supplementary Table S11). H6 partitions were reconstructed with specified seeds. Code inspection was confined to two accessible examples with identifiable selection paths: A4's validation/test loader and B1's ensemble-selection routines. In A5, candidate predictions and IC50 values were compared with Supporting Tables S3–S5, and both designed libraries were matched exactly against the audited F665 files (Supplementary Tables S9 and S9b). For five candidate studies, starting libraries, filtered sets and assayed peptides were extracted as separate counts from the original papers (Supplementary Table S10). The code inspection was illustrative rather than a survey of the remaining studies; no predictor was retrained. Source hashes, software versions, code locations and reconstruction steps are supplied in the Supplementary Material.

To characterise a food-related source feature in F665, the archived A3 positive and negative files were matched against six reviewed bovine-milk protein sequences from UniProt (The UniProt Consortium, 2025). For each class, exact contiguous matches were counted among distinct peptides of at least five residues, limiting incidental short-peptide matches. Length strata and a six-residue threshold were used as checks. The seven identities assigned both labels occur once in each class and were retained in the class-specific counts. Frozen protein sequences, their retrieval manifest, four A3 input hashes, the per-peptide results and the recomputation script accompany Supplementary Table S8.

# 3. What a negative label means in food-peptide prediction

## 3.1 What activity resources record

An activity database records what has been reported for a peptide, not every function for which that peptide has been tested. BIOPEP-UWM links peptide sequences to activities and publications and distinguishes numerical potency measurements from entries without a value (Minkiewicz et al., 2019). DFBP contains 6,276 activity entries for 3,997 distinct sequences and connects food-peptide activities with their possible protein precursors (Qin et al., 2022). Multiple entries may therefore describe one sequence, while a missing DPP-IV entry says nothing about whether DPP-IV was assayed. If these resources supply model labels, the measured endpoint, assay conditions and source record determine what each label can mean. Counting database entries as independent peptides, or treating an absent annotation as an inactive result, changes the task before a model is trained.

The recorded endpoint also changes across specialised resources. The Milk Bioactive Peptide Database associates milk-peptide sequences with protein precursors and reported activities, whereas BioDADPep organises peptides linked to diabetes types and targets (Nielsen et al., 2017; Roy and Teron, 2019). A match to a precursor identifies a possible source sequence; it does not supply an enzyme-assay result. Likewise, ADP-Fuse (A2) first distinguishes antidiabetic from other peptides and then assigns diabetes-type labels, while the T1DM/T2DM predictor (A8) classifies between disease categories (Basith et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024). A negative label for one category in the latter task cannot be read as a measured failure to inhibit DPP-IV. Another reported function gives evidence for that function; a recorded DPP-IV non-response gives evidence for DPP-IV under its stated assay and threshold. Missing DPP-IV annotation establishes neither (Minkiewicz et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2027). Table 1 separates these sources of an assigned zero. Their subsequent reuse in predictor datasets can be examined only after the labels have been tied to the observations and tasks from which they arose.

## 3.2 Other-function, random and null comparators answer different questions

Using peptides with another recorded function creates a defined classification task. AVPdb and DRAMP contain antiviral and antimicrobial peptides, respectively; SATPdb and THPdb cover other therapeutic peptide records (Qureshi et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2016; Usmani et al., 2017; Kang et al., 2019). AntiDMPpred draws its negative class from AVPdb and describes antidiabetic activity for those peptides as unreported (Chen et al., 2022). One-versus-rest peptide classifiers similarly contrast annotations from different function groups (Bizzotto et al., 2024). A model evaluated on these sets can be judged on separation of the sampled groups. Whether a given member inhibits DPP-IV requires a target-specific assay.

Food peptides make the difference between a function label and target inactivity especially clear. DFBP records several activities for some sequences; an ACE or antioxidant annotation does not preclude DPP-IV inhibition (Qin et al., 2022). Positive-unlabelled learning treats an unrecorded target outcome differently from an observed negative, although inference still depends on how the known positives were collected (Elkan and Noto, 2008). Other-function controls remain legitimate for the classification contrast they define. Their practical limitation appears when a predictor trained on that contrast is used to reject candidates whose DPP-IV activity has never been measured.

Random or generated controls make the sampling rule itself part of the modelled task. iDPPIV-SCM (H1) describes random unique peptides and also uses language implying a lack of DPP-IV inhibition (Charoenkwan et al., 2020). ADP-Fuse (A2) combines randomly sampled and other-function peptides (Basith et al., 2023). The choice has measurable consequences in related prediction settings. Adding untested molecule-target pairs improved one screening benchmark yet slightly reduced results in a temporal evaluation, and antimicrobial-peptide benchmarks vary with the relationship between training and evaluation negatives (Cáceres et al., 2020; Sidorczuk et al., 2022). The construction rule can yield a useful reference comparison, but its negatives have not thereby become peptides with measured inactivity.

“Null” can likewise name a modelling class rather than an assay outcome. The available A12 spreadsheet contains 200 synthetic null peptides, although its mapping to the larger partitions described in the paper remains unresolved (Rahmani Asl et al., 2026). Their generation rule is the evidence available for this class. Length, composition and source restrictions shape how these controls differ from the positives. The same properties may also matter biologically; comparing their distributions with those of the intended candidates is more informative than assuming either a purely artificial or a purely functional distinction. F665 provides a sequence-level example of such a distributional comparison in Section 4.

## 3.3 Experimental inactivity is endpoint- and condition-specific

Measured non-response is stronger evidence than missing annotation only when the test is specified. Target, concentration range, assay conditions, controls and the rule for calling a peptide inactive define what was observed. Nongonierma and FitzGerald (2016) found no significant descriptor-potency relationship when they combined milk-peptide DPP-IV results from different assays; when the assays were matched, they further restricted their QSAR analysis to competitive inhibitors. Converting such results to binary labels does not remove those differences. A below-threshold result in a stated assay can support a negative label for that endpoint. An untested sequence cannot support a measured non-response label. Opposing labels for an identical sequence are directly observable in a sequence-only benchmark; published inhibition measurements for two such sequences are examined in Section 4.3, although the provenance of their negative assignments remains unresolved (Luo et al., 2027). Table 1 summarises the four sources of an assigned negative label. They may overlap for an individual peptide, but they imply different observations and training contrasts. Once the label's meaning is known, deposited sequences and evaluation roles can be checked to see whether apparently separate studies share the same test population.

# 4. How construction, reuse and evaluation define the tested comparison

## 4.1 The sampled contrast can differ from the intended biological task

Negative-set construction determines what a peptide classifier can separate on its benchmark. If the two classes differ in sequence source, length or family membership, these properties can contribute to classification alongside the target activity. Work on shortcut learning and controlled biological benchmarks shows why performance on one collection need not persist when the cues defining that collection change (Geirhos et al., 2020; Eid et al., 2021). Peptide length and composition may themselves be biologically relevant; the issue is whether their distributions in the benchmark reflect the task for which the predictor will be used. In antimicrobial-peptide prediction, experimentally varying the negative-sampling procedure and its relation to evaluation data altered benchmark results (Sidorczuk et al., 2022). For antidiabetic peptides, the relevant comparison is likewise between the evaluated negatives and the candidates from which a screening decision will be made.

The F665 files show how different the labelled populations can be before any model is fitted. In the archived StackDPPIV copy, 665 unique positives have a median length of four residues and 665 unique negatives a median of 15; 352 positives, but no negatives, are at most four residues long. Among peptides of at least five residues, 198 of 313 positives and 59 of 665 negatives are exact substrings of six selected bovine-milk proteins (Supplementary Table S8; The UniProt Consortium, 2025). Seven sequences appear in both classes and all seven match the beta-casein reference P02666. These are differences in deposited sequence properties and reference matches, not measurements of each peptide's origin or of what a fitted predictor learned. They matter when the intended candidates are short designed peptides or food-peptide lists with a different composition: a score on F665 need not describe selection within those libraries.

Related work has tested whether changing the evaluated population alters an apparent model advantage. In the ADP-Hybrid preprint, candidate precursors were assigned to 218 of 877 ADP-Fuse positives: 140 human-insulin fragments labelled type 1, 76 bovine-milk-protein fragments labelled type 2 and two human-amylin fragments (Islam et al., 2026, preprint). The same study examined 16 other peptide benchmarks and found excess within-family label sharing in 11 of 14 testable datasets. Under its homology-aware reconstruction of ADP-Fuse, an architectural advantage seen with random partitions disappeared. Those are performance results of the preprint's reconstruction. The F665 analysis here instead reports differences in released sequences and roles, without training or comparing the predictors whose files were audited.

Simple-feature baselines make these distributional differences testable as model inputs. Comparisons across nine peptide-function groups show that group size and sequence properties vary across tasks, while local molecular fingerprints have performed strongly across several peptide datasets (Bizzotto et al., 2024; Adamczyk et al., 2026). A length-only or composition-only baseline can show how much of a particular benchmark is separable by those features. A stronger predictor could then be compared under source-aware or homology-aware partitions to determine whether its advantage persists when sequence dependencies change. This proposal follows from the observed F665 distributions and negative-sampling studies; it is an evaluation design, not a result of the present sequence audit (Sidorczuk et al., 2022).

## 4.2 Exact benchmark reuse and version relationships

Exact sequence matches reveal extensive reuse of one negative reference set (Figure 2). Negative files were retrievable for 17 of 21 audited studies and complete for 14 identified versions; the corresponding counts in the original 20-study sample were 16 and 13. Three records remain partial or unresolved, and four are source-linked, inaccessible or noncomparable (A6, A8, A10 and A11; Supplementary Table S3). Seven inspected versions contain the same 665 negatives and the same 532 negative training members and 133 negative test members. Four are iDPPIV-SCM (H1), iDPPIV-SI (H2), iDPPIV-PC (H3) and IPPF-FE (H5) (Charoenkwan et al., 2020; Zou, 2024; Zou and Yin, 2021; Yu and Luo, 2023). StackDPPIV (A3), StructuralDPPIV (A4) and BERT-DPPIV (A5) complete the group (Charoenkwan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2023). The A3 copy was recovered from an archive and A5 from an author repository; several authors also acknowledge reuse. These versions therefore do not supply seven independent negative test populations. The positive training and test members also match H1 in H2, H3, H5, A3 and A5. A4 has the same positive test members but lacks the H1 positive training dipeptide NA: sharing the negative benchmark does not make all seven full training files identical (Supplementary Table S6). A4's reported test count also differs from its accessible file, as discussed in Section 4.5.

Membership and split identity must be checked separately for related versions (Table 2). SeqDPPIV (H6) provides its complete 629-negative model input: precisely the F665 negatives of at most 33 residues, subsequently divided using its own random split (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026). BBATProt (H4) provides 532 training negatives identical to those in the H1 and H3 training files, while the members of its reported 133-negative test set were unavailable in the inspected release (Wang et al., 2025). H3's test file is a candidate source for those members, not an observed H4 test partition.

The repository released by Yue et al. (A7) contains 4,200 training and 1,050 test negatives, whereas the paper describes retaining 3,970 of an initial 4,000 training negatives (Yue et al., 2024). Without a mapping between these descriptions, the accessible repository set cannot be assigned the paper's reported evaluation role. Terminal `X` padding is documented for 4,189 repository training negatives. Once that padding is removed, the repository set shares 51 exact identities with F665, 101 with F966, 120 with the complete ADP-Fuse (A2) set and 205 with AntiDMPpred (A1) (Figure 3). These counts describe exact matches in the normalised repository files; they do not include related but nonidentical sequences or common protein precursors (Whalen et al., 2022).

## 4.3 Opposite labels retained and removed across versions

Seven sequences carry both positive and negative labels in the files shared by the seven fixed-partition F665 versions. Four (GPFPIIV, LHLPLP, LPVPQ and LTQTPVVVPPF) appear in both training classes. The other three also occur across the training and test sets: FAQTQS is a training negative and test positive, while LTDVEN and VRGPFP are training positives and test negatives (Table 3). These seven identities represent 1.1% of the 665 unique negatives; three have an occurrence among the 266 test records. The 49 sequence-by-version occurrences reflect repeated retention of seven identities, not 49 different conflicting peptides. Each sequence exactly matches bovine beta-casein in the bounded protein lookup (Section 4.1). The deposited labels conflict for a sequence-only target. In original assays, LPVPQ inhibited DPP-IV with an IC50 of 43.8 ± 8.8 μM in 2016, before the H1 release, and GPFPIIV with an IC50 of 0.2998 ± 0.03 mg mL−1 in a study published in 2025 (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016, Table 4; Zheng et al., 2025, Table 3; Supplementary Table S11). The inhibition measurements establish activity under their respective assay conditions. The later GPFPIIV result cannot explain an earlier label, and neither publication identifies how the negative labels were assigned.

Later versions treat the seven identities differently. SeqDPPIV (H6) retains all seven in its length-filtered input, though its random split changes their possible evaluation roles (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026; reconstructed partitions in the Supplementary Material). In Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1), all seven are absent from both classes in the accessible training, internal-test and external files (Zhang et al., 2026). The B1 benchmark retains 658 F665 negatives and adds three, while a separate external panel contributes another five negatives. The seven F665 negatives omitted from that benchmark are exactly the seven conflict identities, in keeping with the authors' description of re-curation and their discussion of earlier label conflicts.

The F665-to-B1 comparison establishes a change in the benchmark's members and internal label consistency. Removing an identity assigned both labels resolves that sequence-level conflict but also removes LPVPQ and GPFPIIV, whose inhibition has been measured; removal is not a biological verdict that either class was correct. Activity records linked to source and endpoint are needed to assess the remaining negatives (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016; Zheng et al., 2025). The membership changes can be reported precisely, while their effect on a predictor's score remains an empirical question for a controlled comparison.

## 4.4 Source reuse across development and evaluation roles

The ADP-Fuse–BertADP–FedAntiDiaPep comparison shows a different relationship: sequences move between evaluation roles. ADP-Fuse (A2) and the DQN ensemble (A14) share all 1,399 accessible Layer-1 negatives, including the same 658 training and 741 test members (Basith et al., 2023; Kumar and Singh, 2026). BertADP (A9) contains the 658 A2 training negatives plus 308 of A2's test negatives (Xie et al., 2025). Of those earlier test negatives, 241 enter A9's development pool and 67 its external set; the other 433 are not used. A9's development pool contains 899 negatives and 899 positives (1,798 records) before the paper's reported internal 80/20 split, whose membership was not available. All 219 A2 test positives enter A9's development pool, and none enter its external panel (Supplementary Table S4). FedAntiDiaPep (A13) releases 899-negative training and 67-negative test files identical to A9's development and external negatives, respectively (Shoyshob et al., 2026).

A9 reports reuse of the earlier test pool, and its development and external files have no exact sequence overlap even when both classes are considered (Xie et al., 2025). Thus “external” describes a partition within A9; it does not mean that every member was absent from earlier model studies. The intersections in Supplementary Table S4 establish source and role changes, but files alone cannot reveal whether earlier test outcomes guided a later modelling decision. That second question requires the decision path, as emphasised in guidance on model evaluation and data leakage (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023; Walsh et al., 2021). Table 2 records the available file roles, and the code examples below examine how particular implementations used their evaluation data.

## 4.5 From sample separation to decision separation

Holding out records from parameter fitting does not by itself keep their outcomes out of model selection. Checkpoint choice, early stopping, threshold tuning and ensemble comparison can each use labels from an evaluation file. DOME and REFORMS treat these decisions as part of the procedure that a reported score must evaluate, while Cawley and Talbot (2010) show how selecting against an evaluation criterion can overfit that criterion (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024). Leakage analyses extend the concern to information crossing other stages of the workflow (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). In released code, the file loaded for each role and the labels used by selection calls provide direct evidence of that information flow.

In the pinned StructuralDPPIV (A4) code, the DPP-IV loader maps `valid` to `test` (Supplementary Table S2). Isolated calls return the same 266 ordered records for both roles, comprising 133 positives and 133 negatives. The training entry point selects a checkpoint by validation accuracy, applies early stopping using validation F1 and then tests the selected checkpoint on those records (released code, `main/train.py`, lines 38 to 44). This accessible path uses the test file for selection as well as testing. The paper reports 113 test examples per class (Wang et al., 2024), whereas the available file contains 133 per class. Without a mapping between the reported runs and this file, the inspected path cannot be assigned to every published score.

The pinned Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1) implementation uses internal test outcomes in three distinct selection paths (Supplementary Table S2; Zhang et al., 2026). Its stacking branch fits a meta-model on test labels, a step the authors' code flags as prone to leakage. Another branch optimises a threshold against test MCC, while a method-comparison block selects the best ensemble by the same test metric (`CNN_bimodal_Final.py`, lines 555 to 575 and 643 to 672). The paper describes comparison of internal-test ensemble results before selecting its reported average strategy. That final `avg` predictor differs from the stacking fit, though the strategy choice still uses internal test outcomes. A separate external panel of 44 positives and five negatives has no exact sequence overlap with the accessible internal files. The two inspected implementations thus establish particular routes by which evaluation data inform decisions; neither establishes how much any published score would change under another procedure.

# 5. From benchmark accuracy to peptide selection

An assay establishes activity for the peptide tested, whether the candidate was designed for synthesis, predicted from a precursor or identified in a hydrolysate. Screening also asks whether the predictor helped choose active peptides from those eligible for testing. Assaying only the preferred shortlist gives a yield for that shortlist and leaves activity among rejected candidates unknown. The designed-peptide experiments of Guan et al. (2023) and the hydrolysate study of Cournoyer et al. (2025) make the difference visible because their assayed groups include peptides outside a DPP-IV-positive prediction.

## 5.1 Measured activity and missed candidates

BERT-DPPIV (A5) connects the shared F665 benchmark to a published synthesis study: its released files reproduce both F665 membership and the 532/133 negative split. Guan et al. (2023) designed 80 tripeptides in four families and predicted every member to be active. Seventy-two had a measured IC50, while the remaining eight had no detectable IC50 under the reported assay. Eighteen tripeptides also occur among F665 positives, 12 in training and six in test files (Supplementary Table S9b). Thus the 72/80 result describes measured responses among positively predicted designs, some of which were already represented in the benchmark. Because no tripeptide in this set was predicted negative, that experiment cannot show which active tripeptides the model would have rejected.

The same study's inhibitor-enriched, five-residue design library gives a more direct test of the selection rule. None of its 22 designs exactly matches a positive or negative F665 sequence. Fourteen were predicted to inhibit DPP-IV; all eight predicted negatives nevertheless had reported IC50 values indicating activity (Guan et al., 2023, Supporting Tables S4–S5; Supplementary Table S9). IPIPI, one of those eight, had the lowest IC50 in the 22-peptide set at 47.47 μM. The original authors identified these eight as false negatives. Because all 22 designs were assayed, 14/22 counts the model's positive calls among peptides with quantifiable inhibition in this designed library; no potency threshold was fixed for estimating recovery in a food-peptide library. A later study applied StructuralDPPIV (A4) to these published assays and reported 21/22 positive predictions (Wang et al., 2024, p. 6). That comparison is retrospective, uses the same peptide set and cannot isolate the effect of negative-set construction; A4's accessible negative files match A5, while its positive training file differs by one member. Guan et al. note that repeated dipeptide units in the designs were absent from their training set. The assays establish missed active candidates in this design library, without explaining why the models differed.

A measured food-peptide library shows a related, but more restricted, result. Cournoyer et al. (2025) identified 407 sequences in duckweed hydrolysates, then used multivariate analysis, QSAR and several activity endpoints to choose 20 for synthesis. All 20 underwent DPP-IV assays; five inhibited the enzyme. Four of the ten described as predicted DPP-IV inhibitors were active in both enzyme and cell assays. Pep2 entered the selected group partly for its antioxidant interest; the authors report that their DPP-IV QSAR did not predict activity, yet measured a cell-free IC50 of 253.23 ± 13.14 μM (pp. 7–9, Table 4). Its sequence was withheld for a patent application, and the QSAR table gives `n.d.` rather than a numerical predicted IC50, so the individual prediction cannot be independently reproduced. A shortlist assembled across functions thus revealed an active peptide outside the QSAR's reported active group. The observation concerns the selected 20; the other 387 identified peptides were not assayed individually for DPP-IV, so a recovery rate for the full measured hydrolysate library cannot be calculated.

## 5.2 Where the candidate pool narrows

In most food-peptide screens, prediction is only one of several filters between the starting library and the assay plate. The denominator therefore changes at each step. Zhang et al. (2024) enumerated 737 sequences by virtual cleavage of bovine milk proteins and retained 37 after LightGBM, bioactivity, absorption and toxicity filters. Three were randomly chosen *from the 37* for enzyme assays: GPVRGPF and HPHPHL inhibited DPP-IV with IC50 values of 645 and 1,614 μM, while FVAPFPEV showed poor inhibition and no reported IC50. This experiment sampled the filtered group, leaving 34 of its members and all 700 previously discarded sequences without comparable assays. Random selection of three within the shortlist supports a statement about that shortlist, not the 737-sequence starting library (Zhang et al., 2024, p. 4).

The same attrition occurs in screens that start from an experimentally produced hydrolysate. After processing measured highland-barley hydrolysates, Bao et al. (2025) identified 52,988 peptide sequences shorter than 20 residues. GBDT scores followed by length and physicochemical criteria reduced these to 81 candidates; docking narrowed the reported list to eight, and three were synthesised. All three inhibited DPP-IV, with IC50 values of 281.76–766.80 μM. Those assays confirm the activity of the three measured peptides. The study does not report DPP-IV responses for the other five docked peptides, the remaining 73 candidates or the sequences excluded before prediction. In particular, describing low-scored sequences as unlikely inhibitors remains a model-based assessment until some of those sequences are assayed.

Virtual cleavage of rice proteins yielded another narrow assay denominator. Cheng et al. (2025) enumerated 638 distinct peptides, retained 101 for docking after activity and modelling filters, and synthesised four for concentration-response assays. Three inhibited DPP-IV; CPPPPAAY showed no significant inhibition in the tested range. This non-response helps characterise the selected four, but CPPPPAAY still passed the filtering steps and was not a low-priority candidate rejected by the pipeline. Milk, barley and rice studies thus reach the same evidential position from different starting materials: they measure activity in a late shortlist while leaving the earlier excluded sequences unmeasured. Their reported hit rates should be interpreted within each selection procedure, because the starting libraries, filters and assay conditions differ (Supplementary Table S10).

## 5.3 Testing the intended screening comparison

The intended candidate library should determine the evaluation population. The 22 assayed BERT-DPPIV designs were all five residues long; F665 contains 38 positive and 64 negative sequences of that length (Supplementary Table S8; Guan et al., 2023). The model assigned both positive and negative calls within the same-length design library, so length alone cannot distinguish its eight missed inhibitors from the fourteen it selected. The designs were deliberately inhibitor-enriched, and none exactly matched F665: neither the length counts nor the assay results identify why these predictions differed. In the duckweed study, one active peptide outside the reported DPP-IV QSAR-positive group was tested because the selection considered another endpoint. By contrast, the milk, barley and rice studies did not assay peptides excluded before their final shortlists (Cournoyer et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Bao et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025). The common unanswered question is how each selection rule performs among the candidates available before filtering.

A direct test would fix the candidate library, endpoint and selection rule before peptide activities are known. Assaying prespecified samples from both high- and low-ranked groups under the same DPP-IV conditions would reveal yield in the preferred group and active peptides left behind in the rejected group. If the full library is too large, the study can report how many peptides were eligible and sampled in each score range. A library-wide recovery estimate would then require sampling weights that reflect the different probabilities of selection for assay. An alternative ranking or an unranked sample from the same library provides a comparison for the screening rule. None of these design features depends on whether the library was synthesised, obtained by virtual cleavage or observed in a hydrolysate. Assay blanks and reference inhibitors address the quality of the enzyme measurement; assays of lower-ranked peptides address the quality of candidate selection (Walsh et al., 2021; Cawley and Talbot, 2010).

The assay endpoint has to match the result claimed for the selection rule. A binary DPP-IV label cannot measure error in predicted IC50 values or establish a response in cells, even for the same peptide (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016; Zan et al., 2023). Cournoyer et al. (2025) measured both enzyme inhibition and cellular activity for selected duckweed peptides, reporting evidence at the two experimental levels. A screen for active peptides calls for recovery and yield within the defined library; prioritisation calls for agreement between ranking and measured activity; potency prediction requires concentration-response measurements. These outcomes need different comparators, but each can be measured on candidates chosen independently of the outcome being assessed.

Candidate origin defines the population to which a screening result applies. An exact sequence match to a milk protein identifies possible precursor containment, but says nothing about whether a particular digest releases that peptide. Synthesis can establish inhibitory activity without resolving food release. Observed hydrolysate lists, such as those from barley and duckweed, thus define a different candidate population from the virtual-cleavage lists used for milk and rice (Bao et al., 2025; Cournoyer et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025). The route matters when a claim concerns occurrence in food. For the narrower question of which active peptides a predictor selects, the essential denominator is the stated library of eligible sequences, regardless of how that library was assembled.

# 6. What the evidence permits a screening user to conclude

## 6.1 Interpreting existing scores

The seven F665 versions share the same negative test members and their fixed 532/133 negative split; this is one reused negative test population, not seven independent cohorts. H1, H2, H3, H5, A3 and A5 also share the positive partitions, while A4 lacks one positive training member but retains the same positive test members (Supplementary Table S6). Scores from these studies need to be read with the full data and evaluation procedure of each release; shared negative membership does not make all model comparisons identical. The seven opposite-label identities are common to these versions, and published assays establish inhibitory activity for two of them. H6's length filtering and B1's re-curation change the members being compared, so score differences across those versions cannot be attributed to modelling alone. Table 4 relates each relationship to the comparison it can support. Dataset and evaluation reporting follow DOME and REFORMS (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024).

A reused sequence and a reused test outcome pose different problems. A9 explicitly reports drawing from A2 test files; the file intersections show where those sequences reappear but cannot establish whether earlier results influenced A9's model choices. For A4 and B1, inspected code provides direct evidence that internal test records or labels enter particular selection paths. B1's separately released external panel has no exact overlap with its internal files. The practical implication for using a score to choose a predictor is to fix selection decisions before the outcomes of a new candidate cohort are inspected (Cawley and Talbot, 2010; Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). That requirement follows from how the score was obtained, rather than from sequence overlap alone.

## 6.2 Comparisons that answer the remaining question

For those building a benchmark, the immediate requirement is to retain the source of each assigned zero: an untested target, another measured function, a generation rule or a specified non-response assay. The F665 and B1 releases show why version records matter. B1 removes the seven identities with opposite labels, adds three benchmark negatives and places another five negatives in an external panel. The observed sequence changes can be isolated experimentally by holding the positive class, algorithm, partition policy and selection rule fixed while varying negative construction (Sidorczuk et al., 2022; Walsh et al., 2021). That comparison would estimate an effect on model evaluation; the present membership audit identifies the relevant sets but has not run it.

For researchers using an existing predictor, the informative comparison is measured DPP-IV activity across its intended candidate library. Assaying all 22 A5 pentapeptides showed that all eight assigned negative predictions were active; the duckweed study reported an active peptide for which its DPP-IV QSAR did not predict activity, although the undisclosed sequence precludes reproducing that prediction (Guan et al., 2023; Cournoyer et al., 2025). Milk, barley and rice studies show a different design: assays followed successive filters, leaving the fate of earlier excluded sequences unknown (Zhang et al., 2024; Bao et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025). Recording the size of each eligible group and assaying some lower-ranked candidates would make it possible to evaluate the selection decision. Whether those candidates were synthesised or obtained from food determines the population to which the result applies, while the peptide's measured activity remains an experimental observation in its own right.

## 6.3 Scope and unresolved records

The audited predictors form a purposeful sample, and code inspection covers two implementations with accessible decision paths. Several source boundaries remain material: H4's test members were unavailable; A7's repository files cannot be mapped fully to the paper; A12's complete reported corpus was not recovered; and A10's stated download link was inaccessible at the cutoff. Exact matches to six milk-protein references establish sequence containment, without identifying the origin of deposited F665 peptides. None of the models was retrained, so the numerical effect of resolving label conflicts on published scores remains unmeasured. Other paper/file discrepancies are documented in Supplementary Table S6, including 531 positive training records for A4 and 839 positive benchmark records representing 838 identities for B1.

# 7. Conclusions

Across the examined antidiabetic-peptide predictors, the negative class varied in meaning and in its subsequent use. Seven versions reused the same F665 negative members and split, including seven sequences assigned opposing labels. Other versions filtered or revised that set, while sequences formerly used for testing entered later development and external files. These relationships specify what the released benchmark comparisons contain, without estimating how re-curation would change model performance. Experimental selection provides a complementary result: in BERT-DPPIV's complete 22-peptide design, all eight peptides predicted negative showed inhibitory activity, whereas several food-protein studies assayed only late shortlists. Confirmed hits in those shortlists establish peptide activity, but selection across their original libraries remains unmeasured. For screening, the next informative comparison is therefore within a defined candidate library, using the same assay for peptides the rule favours and a prespecified sample it would reject. The same comparison applies to both designed and food-derived candidate libraries, provided the resulting claim remains tied to the library and endpoint actually tested.
