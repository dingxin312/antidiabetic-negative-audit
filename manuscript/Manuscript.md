# Negative evidence in AI-assisted discovery of food-derived antidiabetic peptides: Construction, benchmark reuse and validation boundaries

## Abstract

**Background:** Antidiabetic peptide predictors combine negative labels drawn from experimental non-response, missing annotations and constructed controls. Each source defines a different comparison, with consequences for selecting peptides from food proteins.

**Scope and approach:** This critical review combines a provenance audit of 21 selected prediction studies with methodological research and food-peptide experiments. It follows negative evidence from label construction through benchmark reuse and model evaluation to the experimental comparisons needed for food screening.

**Key findings and conclusions:** Seven accessible benchmark versions contain the same 665 negatives and the same 532/133 negative file partition; seven sequences have opposing labels. A length-filtered input retains these conflicts, whereas a re-curated benchmark removes them. Another lineage reallocates earlier test negatives between later development and external sets. Food-peptide experiments further show how candidate selection, assay endpoint and processed-matrix controls change the question answered by a result. A useful screening claim therefore connects its negative-set version and evaluation role to a defined candidate population, measured endpoint and food-use comparator. Together, these findings define an evidence boundary, not a field-wide performance estimate.

**Keywords:** food-derived peptides; antidiabetic activity; negative samples; benchmark provenance; label uncertainty; validation

# 1. Introduction

Food proteins supply a large peptide sequence space through enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation and digestion. Computational screening can narrow the candidates for synthesis and testing, while laboratory studies must still identify whether an observed activity belongs to an intact protein, a hydrolysate, a fraction or an individual peptide. Reviews have described the data, representation, model and validation workflow and its applications to food-peptide discovery (Chang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). In practice, BERT-DPPIV guided synthesis and inhibition measurements, machine-learning and virtual-proteolysis strategies identified bovine-milk candidates, and a duckweed study combined peptide profiling with modelling and tests of several activities (Guan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Cournoyer et al., 2025). These examples put a specific question at the centre of AI-assisted discovery: the evidence that connects a model's predicted category to the food-derived material and endpoint that researchers actually test.

The negative class is a particularly consequential point of translation. A peptide below an experimental activity threshold, a sequence absent from an activity database, an antiviral peptide used as a comparator and a randomly generated sequence may all receive a zero in a training file. Yet AntiDMPpred describes its antiviral-source negatives as having no reported antidiabetic activity, while ADP-Fuse combines random and other-function peptides (Chen et al., 2022; Basith et al., 2023; Qureshi et al., 2014). Learning from positive and unlabelled examples learning similarly distinguishes an unrecorded label from a confirmed negative (Elkan and Noto, 2008). Constructed comparators can still improve a particular modelling task: stochastic negative addition helped one molecular-screening benchmark but reduced performance on a temporal benchmark (Cáceres et al., 2020). What matters is the contrast created by a negative-sampling rule and the decision for which that contrast is being used.

The surrounding literature addresses different parts of this translation. Chang et al. organize AI-assisted food-peptide screening by workflow and application; Zhao et al. examine methodological limitations (Chang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Luo et al. investigate how test conditions and separate functional outputs enter peptide models, while Yang et al. examine model applicability under food-matrix, task and deployment conditions (Luo et al., 2027; Yang et al., 2026). Alaei et al. appraise evidence for operational claims in food processing, and the ADP-Hybrid preprint studies homology-aware splits and source-associated patterns in one antidiabetic benchmark (Alaei et al., 2026; Islam et al., 2026, preprint). Together, these works make it possible to ask how the origins and subsequent evaluation roles of negative labels shape the comparison offered to food-peptide researchers.

DOME and REFORMS distinguish data provenance, optimization decisions and evaluation design, and biological benchmarks show that sampling and partitioning deserve scrutiny alongside architecture (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024; Sidorczuk et al., 2022). We follow three connected questions. Training labels determine what groups a model learns to distinguish; the evaluation procedure determines what its reported score measures; and a food experiment supplies the observations and controls for the intended use. Table 1 locates each kind of comparator at its analytical level. The provenance audit addresses the first two questions, while experimental studies show how the third is answered for selected peptides, assays and food materials. A weakness in a benchmark changes the interpretation of its score; a separately controlled food experiment stands on the evidence generated by that experiment.

# 2. Review scope and evidence approach

This review combines focused critical synthesis with a bounded provenance audit. Supporting literature came from the project's existing food-peptide and modelling library and citation tracking to primary papers. We selected sources that clarify label meaning, negative sampling, evaluation design or evidence for a defined screening use. Database papers, methodological studies and food experiments provide different kinds of support; the food examples were selected to compare candidate choice, assay and material context, and processing or matrix effects. This is a purposeful selection rather than a systematic survey. The literature and audit cutoff was 2 November 2026; later issue years refer to papers already available online, and preprints are identified. Supplementary Table S1 records each source and the observation used in the synthesis.

The 20 primary audit records, A1 to A14 and H1 to H6, came from the project's existing DPP-IV/ADP model list. Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1) was added post hoc to examine re-curation and external testing. All 21 records are mapped to their task in Supplementary Table S3 and to source artifacts in Supplementary Table S2. Records remained in scope even when their negative files were inaccessible or their tasks differed: inclusion and sequence verification answer separate questions. General antidiabetic activity, DPP-IV inhibition, diabetes-type classification and residue-level prediction were handled as distinct targets (Basith et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024; Basith et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026, preprint). The sequence comparisons concern identifiable accessible versions, including an archived A3 copy, author-repository A5 files, the H6 model input and a pinned B1 implementation. Non-food records illuminate resources and methods used for this prediction area; the food experiments in Section 5 address application to food-derived candidates.

We recorded three audit dimensions: completeness of an identified negative-sequence version, its relationship to a reference set and its negative split identity (Figure 1). Completeness means that the version's full negative membership was obtained; partial includes an unresolved paper-to-file correspondence; and not verified covers source links, unavailable files and noncomparable tasks. Relationship records exact identity, containment, filtering or re-curation relative to F665 or F966, names for reference sets rather than biological lineages. Split identity compares the members of accessible training and test files with H1 or records a study-specific split, development pool or incomplete partition. Thus, H6's full 629-member input is complete despite being a length-filtered F665 subset; A9's 899 development negatives belong to a 1,798-record positive-plus-negative pool, with final internal split membership unverified (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026; Xie et al., 2025). A6 remains source-linked to A2 rather than counted as a verified independent deposit (Arshad et al., 2024). Counts taken from papers, accessible files and verified complete versions are reported separately.

We parsed files while retaining original sequences, normalized sequences and source locators. Normalization used uppercase conversion and whitespace removal, with documented terminal `X` padding removed only for A7; the valid dipeptide `NA` was preserved. We kept record counts separate from unique identities and compared complete sequence identities, opposite labels and cross-label training/test intersections as distinct calculations. H6 splits were reconstructed with stratified `train_test_split`, `test_size=0.2` and seeds 1 to 10; the software environment is recorded in the Supplementary Material. For A4 we executed isolated loader functions and inspected the checkpoint-selection calls; for B1 we inspected a pinned implementation. No predictor was retrained. Cross-checks, source hashes, scripts and reconstruction results are documented in the Supplementary Material and public audit repository; disagreements between paper text and accessible files are reported at the point of interpretation.

# 3. What a negative label means in food-peptide prediction

## 3.1 What activity resources record

Food-peptide databases organize observations and annotations rather than a complete inventory of active and inactive sequences. BIOPEP-UWM links peptides to activities and bibliographic sources and distinguishes measured potency from records without a numerical value (Minkiewicz et al., 2019). DFBP reports 6,276 activity entries representing 3,997 distinct sequences and supports multifunctional analysis and tracing peptides to food proteins (Qin et al., 2022). When these records enter a prediction dataset, the unit of analysis matters: activity entries can duplicate a sequence, and absence from a functional category may reflect the scope of testing. The measured function and its experimental conditions must therefore travel with the source record. That provenance determines whether a missing target annotation is an unlabelled observation or a defensible source for a particular comparator group.

Specialized resources provide different parts of that provenance. The Milk Bioactive Peptide Database focuses on experimentally reported milk-protein peptides, BioPepDB integrates food-derived bioactive-peptide information, and FermFooDb connects fermented-food sequences to food source, fermentation conditions and testing models (Nielsen et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Chaudhary et al., 2021). A milk entry can link a sequence to a precursor and an activity study; a fermentation record can locate the process that yielded it. These fields support source-aware dataset construction, while unlisted peptide-endpoint combinations remain unmeasured unless the underlying experiments say otherwise. Researchers can represent that missing evidence through a declared sampling convention, an unlabelled-data approach or targeted additional testing. The choice changes what the trained model learns to distinguish and should be made with the screening population in view.

Sequence provenance and functional evidence also answer different questions. BioDADPep assembles peptides associated with type 1 and type 2 diabetes and their targets; UniProt can help identify candidate protein precursors (Roy and Teron, 2019; The UniProt Consortium, 2025). ADP-Fuse (A2) first classifies ADPs versus non-ADPs and then separates diabetes-type labels, whereas the T1DM/T2DM classifier (A8) uses disease categories directly (Basith et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024). A zero in a diabetes-type task can designate the other disease category, with a meaning quite different from an experimentally inactive DPP-IV peptide. Similarly, finding a sequence within a food protein establishes a possible precursor, while release and concentration depend on processing and digestion. Keeping disease association, precursor identity and measured inhibition as separate attributes prevents a single binary label from obscuring the task.

## 3.2 Other-function, random and null comparators answer different questions

Other-function peptides provide experimentally studied comparator sequences. AVPdb records antiviral peptides, DRAMP antimicrobial peptides, and SATPdb and THPdb broader therapeutic sequences or approved peptide and protein products (Qureshi et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2016; Usmani et al., 2017; Kang et al., 2019). AntiDMPpred draws negatives from AVPdb and describes their antidiabetic activity as unreported (Chen et al., 2022). Bizzotto et al. instead build one-versus-rest tasks from other annotated functional groups (Bizzotto et al., 2024). Both are interpretable classification contrasts when the sampling rule is declared. They ask whether a model separates the selected annotation groups; an assay of target-enzyme inhibition asks a different question about a particular peptide's response.

Multifunctionality makes the distinction observable. Cournoyer et al. tested duckweed peptides across DPP-IV inhibition, ACE inhibition and antioxidant assays, and DFBP accommodates multiple activities for one sequence (Cournoyer et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2022). An other-function label thus carries positive evidence for its recorded function while leaving target activity to be measured. Learning from positive and unlabelled examples learning formalizes the related difference between an unrecorded class label and the underlying class (Elkan and Noto, 2008). Applying such an approach would require attention to how the known positives were selected. For food-peptide screening, the immediate design choice is clear: an other-function comparator supports separation of sampled groups, while target-specific inhibition requires target-specific observations.

Randomly sampled and generated controls define a contrast through their construction rule. iDPPIV-SCM (H1) describes random unique peptides alongside functional non-inhibitory wording, while ADP-Fuse (A2) combines random and other-function components (Charoenkwan et al., 2020; Basith et al., 2023). In molecular screening, stochastic addition of untested molecule-target pairs improved one benchmark but slightly reduced performance on a temporal benchmark (Cáceres et al., 2020). Antimicrobial-peptide benchmarks likewise respond to the negative-sampling procedure and to how training and evaluation negatives relate (Sidorczuk et al., 2022). The modelling value of constructed controls therefore depends on the task and evaluation population. Stating how they were sampled gives a score an interpretable reference without presenting an untested sequence as a measured biological non-responder.

The term “null” also describes an assigned modelling role. The accessible A12 spreadsheet contains 200 synthetic null peptides, with correspondence to the paper's larger training and evaluation partitions unresolved (Rahmani Asl et al., 2026). Their construction rule, rather than an individual DPP-IV assay, is the available evidence for those records. Sequence length, residue composition, source restrictions and separation from the positives determine the contrast a generated comparator presents to a classifier. For example, controls sampled outside the length range of likely food digests could make length a useful discriminator even if the intended screening task concerns inhibition among peptides of similar length. Documenting the sampling domain makes such results interpretable against the intended candidate population.

## 3.3 Experimental inactivity is endpoint- and condition-specific

An experimental non-response is defined by its endpoint, concentration range, decision criterion, assay sensitivity and controls. The milk-peptide QSAR study shows how differences in assay context complicate the combination of measured potencies (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016); Section 5.2 examines the consequences for ranking and numerical prediction. Peptide identity can also change during measurement: incubation with DPP-IV has been used to distinguish substrate-type, prodrug-type and true-inhibitor behaviour, involving parent molecules and their products (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2014). Section 5.3 returns to digestion and processing. These studies make the experimental conditions part of the label's meaning. Where those conditions have been discarded, an opposing label for the same sequence is an observable inconsistency in the sequence-only target, while its biological explanation remains a separate investigation.

Table 1 brings these sources of negative evidence together at their respective analytical levels. A database-unannotated record represents missing target evidence; an other-function record has evidence for a different activity; a generated record has a known construction rule; and a measured non-response has a result under stated conditions. The categories may overlap for one sequence and are best retained as attributes of the record, together with its source and intended comparator role. Their value for modelling follows the question posed by a particular candidate population. Section 4 turns from the meaning of an individual negative label to the comparison produced when such labels are sampled, inherited across benchmarks and used in model selection.

# 4. How construction, reuse and evaluation define the tested comparison

## 4.1 The sampled contrast can differ from the intended biological task

A negative-sampling procedure determines the contrast available to a classifier. Differences in source, length, composition and sequence families can separate positive and negative files alongside, or instead of, the intended functional target. Shortcut-learning research explains how a rule can perform well within one collection and fail when its collection-specific cues change; systematic biological audits use controlled comparisons to identify the signal actually learned (Geirhos et al., 2020; Eid et al., 2021). Peptide properties may themselves be biologically relevant, so their presence as predictors is informative only in relation to the task and sampling design. Sidorczuk et al. directly vary negative sampling in antimicrobial prediction and find that benchmark results depend on the relationship between training and evaluation negatives (Sidorczuk et al., 2022). For antidiabetic screening, this points to a concrete evaluation question: whether the tested comparator distribution resembles the candidates among which a decision will be made.

The ADP-Hybrid preprint tests this question in a related benchmark (Islam et al., 2026, preprint). Islam et al. examine source attribution, sequence similarity, length baselines and homology-aware partitions in ADP-Fuse. They assign candidate precursors to 218 of 877 positives; within that subset, 140 human-insulin fragments carry the type-1 label and 76 bovine-milk-protein fragments the type-2 label. Model comparisons also change under their homology-aware evaluation. This is evidence of source-associated structure in the assigned subset, particularly relevant when endogenous and food-related sequences share a classification task. Precursor assignment is incomplete, and these performance comparisons belong to the preprint's reconstruction; the present audit examines sequence membership and roles instead.

Simple-feature baselines offer a way to examine which information a sampled task rewards. Bizzotto et al. compare encodings and classifiers across nine peptide function groups with differing sizes and sequence properties; Adamczyk et al. report strong results for local molecular fingerprints across multiple peptide datasets (Bizzotto et al., 2024; Adamczyk et al., 2026). A length or composition baseline measures how readily the collection can be separated using such features. Comparing a more elaborate model against that baseline under source- or homology-aware partitions then tests whether its advantage persists when dependencies change. Together with negative-sampling experiments, these studies connect model comparison to the candidate population and the specific distribution of the comparator class (Sidorczuk et al., 2022).

## 4.2 Exact benchmark reuse and version relationships

The audit resolves several version relationships at the level of sequence identity (Figure 2). Negative sequences were retrievable for 17 of 21 study records, with 14 complete for their identified version; within the 20 primary records, the counts were 16/20 and 13/20. The remaining statuses comprise three partial or unresolved records and four source-linked, unavailable or noncomparable records (A6, A8, A10 and A11; Supplementary Table S3). Seven audited versions, namely iDPPIV-SCM (H1), iDPPIV-SI (H2), iDPPIV-PC (H3), IPPF-FE (H5), StackDPPIV (A3), StructuralDPPIV (A4) and BERT-DPPIV (A5), contain the same 665 negative identities and the same 532/133 members in their accessible training and test files (Charoenkwan et al., 2020; Zou, 2024; Zou and Yin, 2021; Yu and Luo, 2023; Charoenkwan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2023). A3 was recovered from an archive and A5 from author-repository files. Several papers acknowledge reuse. The exact member matches establish one shared reference comparison across seven examined versions; for A4, the paper's reported test count differs from the accessible file count, as detailed in Section 4.5.

Two related versions show why set membership, completeness and partition identity are recorded separately in Table 2. SeqDPPIV (H6) uses 664 positive and 629 negative input records; the negative members are exactly the F665 sequences of at most 33 residues, followed by its own random split (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026). Its 629-member input is complete as a version even though it is a filtered F665 subset. BBATProt (H4) provides 532 training negatives identical to H1/H3 training members, and reports a 133-member test set that was unavailable in the reviewed release (Wang et al., 2025). The H3 test file is a candidate source link, not an observed H4 test partition; the apparent citation-number mismatch in H4 provides no sequence-level evidence for that partition.

Yue et al. (A7) illustrate how a documented sequence representation changes an overlap calculation (Yue et al., 2024). The repository contains 4,200 training and 1,050 test negatives, whereas the paper describes retaining 3,970 of an initial 4,000 training negatives; their membership correspondence remains unresolved. The repository README specifies terminal `X` padding on 4,189 of the 4,200 training negatives. Removing that documented padding yields exact intersections of 51 with F665, 101 with F966, 120 with the full ADP-Fuse (A2) negative set and 205 with AntiDMPpred (A1) (Figure 3). These intersections belong to the normalized repository version. They show both why raw-string comparison can miss shared identities and why independently sourced benchmarks need to be compared at the sequence level. Exact intersections address identity; near-homology and shared precursor fragments require separate analyses (Whalen et al., 2022).

## 4.3 Inherited labels and changes introduced by re-curation

The seven fixed-partition F665 versions also retain seven sequences assigned opposing labels. Four sequences, GPFPIIV, LHLPLP, LPVPQ and LTQTPVVVPPF, occur in both training classes. Three cross the deposited boundary with opposing labels: FAQTQS is a training negative and test positive, whereas LTDVEN and VRGPFP are training positives and test negatives. Table 3 gives their source coordinates; the supplementary occurrence table records their presence in each version. These are seven conflicting identities represented in 49 sequence-by-version records. Exact reuse carries the same sequence-only label inconsistency into successive benchmark versions, although the underlying assay histories would be needed to adjudicate each biological label.

Length filtering and re-curation have different effects on these identities. The SeqDPPIV (H6) input retains all seven; under the audit's ten specified split reconstructions, nine seeds place one to four conflicting identities across the partition boundary and one places none, while the input identities remain unchanged (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026). The accessible Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1) training, internal-test and external files instead exclude all seven from both classes (Zhang et al., 2026). Its benchmark negatives contain 658 retained F665 identities and three additions, with five further negatives in a separate external panel. The seven F665 negatives removed from that benchmark are precisely the seven conflict identities, in line with B1's reported re-curation and discussion of earlier label conflicts.

This comparison separates a change in membership from the experimental status of the remaining labels. Removing contradictory identities makes a sequence-only benchmark internally more consistent; deciding whether the retained negatives inhibit DPP-IV requires the underlying activity records. DOME emphasizes source, partition and redundancy reporting, and activity databases provide ways to trace records to original studies (Walsh et al., 2021; Minkiewicz et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2022). A version record can therefore identify the excluded members, document any reassigned labels and make clear whether the revised benchmark still tests the earlier comparison. That is the interpretive contribution of re-curation: the changed dataset has a traceable target, rather than a higher score being attributed simply to a new algorithm.

## 4.4 Source reuse across development and evaluation roles

The ADP-Fuse, BertADP and FedAntiDiaPep relationship (A2, A9 and A13) shows how shared identities can acquire new evaluation roles. ADP-Fuse (A2) and the DQN ensemble (A14) share an accessible 1,399-negative Layer-1 set with the same 658/741 training/test members (Basith et al., 2023; Kumar and Singh, 2026). BertADP (A9) uses all 658 A2 training negatives and 308 A2 test negatives: 241 of the latter enter A9 development and 67 enter its external set, leaving 433 A2 test negatives unused (Xie et al., 2025). Its development pool comprises 899 negatives and 899 positives, or 1,798 records, before the internal 80/20 division reported in the paper; the members of that division were not recovered. FedAntiDiaPep (A13) publishes 899-negative training and 67-negative test files that match A9's development and external negatives, respectively (Shoyshob et al., 2026). This is a directly observed chain of set memberships and role assignments.

A9 discloses its use of the earlier test pool, and its own development and external sets have no exact sequence intersection, even when positive and negative labels are combined (Xie et al., 2025). Thus, “external” accurately describes the A9 partition but says nothing about whether those identities had appeared in A2. Table 2 summarizes these role assignments across lineages. Source reuse and information use are separate evaluation questions: the former is established from the sequence files, while the latter requires evidence about the decisions made after evaluation outcomes became available (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023; Walsh et al., 2021). The saved implementations in Section 4.5 illustrate what that additional decision evidence can look like.

## 4.5 From sample separation to decision separation

A test set's identity and its use in decision-making are separate properties of an evaluation. DOME and REFORMS include preprocessing, optimization and model selection in the procedure being assessed (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024). Cawley and Talbot explain how selecting a model against an evaluation criterion can itself overfit that criterion (Cawley and Talbot, 2010). Labels used to choose a checkpoint, threshold or ensemble can therefore influence the reported result even when the chosen predictor was fitted on a separate training file. Leakage reviews describe the wider range of such information flows (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). Inspecting the data path and selection calls makes this question testable in accessible implementations.

In the saved StructuralDPPIV (A4) implementation, the DPP-IV loader maps `valid` to `test`. Isolated reader calls return the same 266 ordered records, 133 positives and 133 negatives, for both roles. The training entry point selects a checkpoint by validation accuracy and then tests that checkpoint on those records (Wang et al., 2024). The inspected code path therefore uses the same accessible file for selection and testing. The paper, however, reports 113 test examples per class; its relationship to the 133-per-class accessible file and the historical runs is unresolved. The result establishes how this saved implementation handles the records, while the paper/file discrepancy prevents attributing the same path to every published score.

The saved Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1) implementation shows three distinct uses of internal test outcomes (Zhang et al., 2026). Its stacking branch fits a meta-model with `test_labels`; the optimized-threshold branch maximizes test MCC; and an ensemble-comparison block selects `best_method` by test MCC before reporting metrics for that method. The paper likewise describes comparing ensemble strategies on the internal test data before selecting the average strategy. The final `avg` strategy is separate from the stacking fit, but its selection still uses internal test results. B1 also reports an external panel of 44 positives and five negatives with no exact overlap with the accessible internal data. That external comparison has a different information boundary from the inspected internal selection operations and a small negative sample; its results must be interpreted on those terms.

# 5. What validation permits a food-peptide screening claim

Moving from a benchmark to food-peptide screening changes three things that determine the meaning of a result. Candidate selection specifies which molecules are observed, assay and material choices specify the response being measured, and processing or matrix comparisons specify the incremental effect relevant to a food use. The experimental literature supplies concrete comparisons at each level. Together, they show how a prediction can guide discovery and which additional observation supports a particular claim.

## 5.1 Candidate confirmation and screening performance

Candidate testing can establish a valuable discovery result. BERT-DPPIV reports synthesis and inhibition testing of selected tripeptides and pentapeptides; studies combining machine learning and virtual proteolysis identify and test bovine-milk and rice-protein candidates (Guan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025). The assays measure the selected molecules under their stated conditions. Estimating how well the predictor sorts an entire screening population calls for a different sampling design: outcomes are needed across the prediction groups, including candidates the pipeline would otherwise discard. When docking or manual filters are added after classifier scoring, the tested shortlist reflects the combined pipeline. Distinguishing discovery yield from classification error follows from specifying both the decision rule and the population on which it is evaluated (Walsh et al., 2021; Cawley and Talbot, 2010).

The choice of experimental comparator follows the question being tested. A reference inhibitor checks assay responsiveness, a substrate-free blank helps establish background signal, and a sampled low-score peptide tests the prediction rule on a candidate expected to score poorly. The rice-peptide study combines machine learning, docking and molecular simulation before testing a selected set (Cheng et al., 2025). Goat-milk studies combine virtual digestion, ranking and synthetic-peptide assays, with differing evidence for occurrence in hydrolysates (M. Liu et al., 2026; Dang et al., 2025). These designs establish whether their pipelines produced active tested candidates. A claim about enrichment or missed actives would additionally need a specified sampling frame and a comparison group selected independently of the shortlist. The distinction lets candidate discovery and model assessment be reported as two complementary outcomes.

The studies combine discovery evidence in different ways. Duckweed hydrolysate profiling was linked to modelling and synthetic-peptide assays across several endpoints (Cournoyer et al., 2025). Oyster and pumpkin-seed studies tested prioritized candidates, while a red-alga study additionally quantified a selected peptide in digestion products (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026; Kumagai et al., 2026). A synthetic-peptide assay measures the selected molecule's response; profiling or targeted quantification places that molecule in its source material; additional assays address additional functions. These are complementary observations about a discovery pipeline. Evaluating how many active peptides the pipeline misses would require measurements on a designed sample beyond its selected hits. Reporting the observations by their purpose preserves the value of a successful discovery while making the reach of the selection rule assessable.

## 5.2 Endpoint, material and assay context change the meaning of a comparison

Ranking and quantitative potency prediction diverged in a milk-peptide QSAR study. Nongonierma and FitzGerald synthesized peptides from a set reported in the human intestinal tract and compared predicted with measured DPP-IV inhibition (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016). The ranking of competitive inhibitors was broadly conserved, while absolute predicted IC50 values disagreed with measurements: one descriptor model tended to underestimate and another to overestimate. This result supports prioritization by order under the studied conditions and calls for direct error assessment before using predicted concentrations. The same study describes variation in assay conditions across reported potency values. A binary activity threshold applied to such measurements retains its experimental context; transferring that label to a new assay requires attention to enzyme preparation, protocol and decision threshold.

Within-study comparisons make changes in endpoint visible. For the chickpea-derived peptide IAIPPGIPYW, Zan et al. report an enzyme-assay IC50 of 12.43 ± 0.48 μM and a Caco-2-cell result of 138.63 ± 14.98 μM; PPGIPYW likewise differs between their enzyme and cell assays (Zan et al., 2023). The numerical difference reflects measurements in different experimental systems. Goat-milk peptides GPFPLL and LPYPY have also been examined through purified-peptide inhibition, digestion-related testing and a cellular experiment (Dang et al., 2025). Together these studies show what an additional experimental layer contributes: a new response under a new set of conditions. A model label constructed from enzyme measurements should retain that source, so a subsequent cellular measurement can be interpreted as evidence about translation rather than another estimate of the same quantity.

The material assayed also determines the unit of evidence. A mass-based IC50 for a hydrolysate measures a mixture; a molar IC50 for a synthetic peptide measures a defined molecular species. The black-bean study reports inhibition by hydrolysate fractions alongside candidate-sequence analyses (Ramirez-Lozano et al., 2025). Probiotic-fermented milk comparisons similarly involve products with multiple peptides and other constituents (Mudgil et al., 2024). Fraction-level activity can support development of a functional ingredient, while identifying the active sequence within that fraction calls for direct attribution. Recording whether an observation concerns a sequence, fraction, hydrolysate or complete food matrix allows experimental controls to match the material tested. It also prevents a mixture response from being assigned to a computationally prioritized constituent solely because that constituent could occur in the mixture.

Tenenbaum et al. directly compare whey protein isolate and hydrolysate after simulated gastrointestinal digestion, measuring recombinant and cellular DPP-IV responses, GLP-1 secretion and intestinal-barrier passage (Tenenbaum et al., 2023). The two digested materials show similar DPP-IV inhibition in the reported comparisons, while the prehydrolysed material has a different peptide distribution and stimulates greater GLP-1 secretion under the tested conditions. Holding the material comparison within one study reveals how a processing change can have different effects on different endpoints. For a predictor trained on a broad “antidiabetic” label, the study identifies a choice that otherwise remains hidden: whether the intended output is enzyme inhibition, a cellular response or another measured property of the processed material.

## 5.3 Processing, digestion and food matrices require their own evidence

Predicted release, measured occurrence and retained activity answer successive questions about a candidate in food. Virtual proteolysis identifies sequences compatible with an assumed cleavage procedure. Kumagai et al. go further: they identify EVYR as a DPP-IV inhibitor and quantify it in pepsin-trypsin digestion products from both dulse powder and extracted protein (Kumagai et al., 2026). The measured peptide contents differ between preparations, connecting candidate identity with a specific processing context. A rice-bran study likewise combines peptide identification, synthesis, cell-based inhibition and digestion-stability observations (Zeng et al., 2026). These experiments make food-source occurrence and post-processing behaviour observable alongside activity. They also define the next comparison precisely: the relevant digest and material must be measured when a claim concerns a peptide produced by processing, rather than merely a sequence compatible with virtual cleavage.

Digestion can transform the candidate itself. Nongonierma and FitzGerald distinguish peptides that lose activity after DPP-IV cleavage from prodrug-type material that yields more active products (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2014). The biological question can therefore shift from persistence of the parent sequence to the identities and activity of its products. Reviews of peptide barrier prediction make a related distinction between a molecular property and direct evidence for passage through a barrier (An et al., 2026). For food screening, measuring the digest, identifying relevant products and attributing activity to them are complementary steps. Their order is set by the claim: predicted stability concerns likely cleavage, a digest assay concerns the mixture's response, and product-level attribution concerns the molecules responsible for that response.

Turalić et al. examine that processing question directly by comparing predicted cleavage sites with in vitro digestion of five previously identified milk-derived DPP-IV inhibitory peptides and an additional cleavage-site validation dataset (Turalić et al., 2025). The peptides were largely stable during the specified three-hour incubations, although several predicted cleavage events were absent from the experiments. This observation evaluates the cleavage prediction and parent-sequence persistence; earlier inhibition evidence supplies the separate activity context. Treating those measurements as distinct makes the study useful for interpreting a virtual digestion pipeline: the model's cleavage predictions can be checked against observed products before activity of a processed sample is assigned to a peptide.

Yao et al. show why the food matrix becomes the comparator in an application experiment (Yao et al., 2026). They incorporated linear LQAFEPLR and a cyclic variant into biscuits, examined heating and simulated digestion, and measured α-glucosidase inhibition and starch-digestion behaviour. Control biscuit digesta already showed 29.2% α-glucosidase inhibition. Fortified digesta showed 39.3% for the linear and 46.9% for the cyclic peptide; only the cyclic comparison was statistically significant against the control for that endpoint. The matched biscuit comparison identifies an incremental response above matrix background. Although the assay target differs from the DPP-IV focus of much of the audit, it illustrates the same evidence requirement at the food-use level: the relevant negative comparator is the processed matrix without the added candidate.

The experimental cases form a coherent set of comparisons rather than a single validation score. Candidate assays test the chosen molecules; measurements across prediction groups test selection performance. Enzyme and cellular measurements test responses at different biological levels, and a pure peptide and hydrolysate are different units of attribution. Observed release connects a candidate to processing, while a matched food matrix measures its added effect against background (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016; Zan et al., 2023; Kumagai et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2026). This is how the negative-evidence question carries through the paper: the comparator used to train a classifier, the samples used to assess it and the control used to evaluate a food ingredient answer connected but distinct questions. A useful study states which one its design addresses.

## 5.4 Safety and additional functions as separate endpoints

Safety and multifunctionality extend the same logic to additional endpoints. A TIFS review treats AI-based food-peptide toxicity prediction as a screening step to prioritize subsequent chemical, cellular and animal testing (X. Liu et al., 2026). The NoiTox preprint examines label noise in peptide-toxicity benchmarks, underscoring the value of tracing the evidence behind a predicted negative toxicity label (Almeida et al., 2026, preprint). A separate analysis of peptide models asks whether individual functional outputs and test conditions are represented in their labels (Luo et al., 2027). In a food-screening pipeline, inhibitory activity, safety, digestive products and other functions therefore each call for their own evidence and controls. This endpoint-specific view connects experimental follow-up to the dataset question rather than treating a high activity score as a general certificate for the ingredient.

# 6. Interpreting negative evidence for a defined screening use

## 6.1 Match the comparison to the intended use

A predictor can be used to compare algorithms, select candidates, estimate potency or guide a food formulation. Each use has a corresponding comparison, summarized in Table 4. A within-benchmark evaluation measures discrimination against its assigned labels; candidate assays measure responses of tested molecules; a matched food experiment measures an incremental effect under specified processing conditions. DOME and REFORMS guide assessment of the data, optimization and evaluation decisions behind a model score, while FAIR addresses the access and reuse needed to inspect those research objects (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024; Wilkinson et al., 2016). Taken together, these principles make the relevant question operational: the choice of which observed comparator supports the decision the researcher intends to make.

## 6.2 Priorities that follow from the evidence

For dataset assembly, the assigned zero should carry the evidence that produced it: absent annotation, another recorded function, a generation rule or a measured non-response with its endpoint, material, conditions and decision threshold. Food-peptide resources already retain some of the relevant context, including precursor proteins, functional records and fermentation conditions (Minkiewicz et al., 2019; Nielsen et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Chaudhary et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2022). Preserving these attributes during export and curation allows a later user to recover the intended target. The F665 and Bilingua comparison adds a versioning requirement: members and labels changed during re-curation should be identified before scores from successive benchmarks are compared. That record tells readers whether an apparent model gain was assessed against the same sequence-level task.

For model evaluation, the target, members, metric and selection procedure together define what a reported score measures. Comparisons across sequence families or sources can be tested with partitions designed around those dependencies (Whalen et al., 2022; Walsh et al., 2021). The ADP-Hybrid reconstruction motivates homology-aware tests, and peptide-fingerprint results motivate simple-feature baselines within the appropriate datasets (Islam et al., 2026, preprint; Adamczyk et al., 2026). The A4 and B1 implementations add a decision-level check: checkpoint, threshold and method selection belong to the evaluated procedure when they use test labels (Cawley and Talbot, 2010). Reporting the selected version and the data flow through those choices connects the numerical result to an auditable evaluation, irrespective of whether the source files themselves have different names.

For candidate screening, the experiment should measure the quantity on which the next decision depends. A ranking claim calls for measured responses across the sampled ordering; an enrichment claim also needs a relevant comparison sample. Numerical potency requires prediction errors against concentration-response measurements made under defined conditions (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016; Zan et al., 2023). A processing claim calls for identification and quantification in the relevant digest (Kumagai et al., 2026), while added food effect calls for a matched processed-matrix comparison (Yao et al., 2026). These choices connect computational selection, chemical measurements and biological response to a specific use rather than imposing a universal series of experimental stages (Liang et al., 2025). Safety and additional functional claims bring their own endpoints and exposure conditions into that design (X. Liu et al., 2026).

## 6.3 Scope of the synthesis and unresolved work

The synthesis draws on selected literature and identified audit versions, rather than a prevalence survey or a new estimate of performance effects. Three source questions remain unresolved: the H4 test members, correspondence between the A7 paper and repository, and the complete A12 corpus. A10's file-access failure records the state of its link at the audit cutoff. Adjudicating the seven conflicting biological labels would require source assays; assessing homology or reproducing historical model runs would require separate analyses. Negative-version completeness also differs from completeness of every other field: the accessible A4 files contain 531 positive training records, and B1's positive benchmark has 839 records representing 838 identities (Supplementary Table S6). These specified boundaries locate where additional evidence would most change the present interpretation.

Three matched comparisons follow directly from the combined evidence. A modelling study could vary negative construction while keeping positive examples and model selection fixed; a screening study could test defined samples from both prioritized and deprioritized candidates; and a food experiment could compare the same candidate in solution, after processing and in a matched matrix. The first examines what the sampled labels teach, the second measures the selection rule against a relevant population, and the third measures how the intended use changes the observed response. The audit provides concrete versions and label conflicts for the first question, while the reviewed food studies illustrate the latter two. Framing each study around its intended decision makes new results cumulative without requiring every project to answer every biological question at once.

# 7. Conclusions

Negative labels built from missing annotations, other recorded functions, construction rules and measured non-responses define different comparisons. The audit identifies exact benchmark reuse, seven inherited opposite-label sequences, their removal in a re-curated version and the reassignment of earlier test identities to later evaluation roles. Saved implementations show why selection decisions must be considered alongside sample separation. Food-peptide experiments then locate the comparison at the point of use: a selected molecule, a defined assay, an observed processed product or an effect against a matched matrix. Reporting the negative set and its version, the role of each evaluation sample and the endpoint of each food experiment creates a traceable path from a model score to the decision it is intended to inform.
