# Negative evidence in AI-assisted discovery of food-derived antidiabetic peptides: Construction, benchmark reuse and validation boundaries

## Abstract

**Background:** Negative labels in antidiabetic peptide prediction can represent experimental non-response, missing annotation or constructed controls. These are different kinds of evidence, but they are often compressed into one class, which limits how a prediction can be interpreted in food-peptide screening.

**Scope and approach:** This critical review examines 21 studies alongside food-peptide experiments and methodological literature. Training labels define the comparison a model learns; evaluation procedures define what a score can support; food-use experiments provide the comparator for a new claim. The audit is bounded and is not a census or a performance meta-analysis.

**Key findings and conclusions:** Seven audited versions share 665 negatives and a 532/133 negative training/test split. The shared F665 benchmark contains seven sequences with opposite labels. A length-filtered input retains these conflicts, whereas a re-curated benchmark removes them; a separate lineage reassigns earlier test negatives to later development and external roles. These findings limit what a benchmark score can replace, but they do not show that performance is inflated or that separate food experiments are invalid. Screening claims should therefore state the candidate population, endpoint and comparator actually represented, and report the version and evaluation role of the negative evidence.

**Keywords:** food-derived peptides; antidiabetic activity; negative samples; benchmark provenance; label uncertainty; validation

# 1. Introduction

AI-assisted peptide discovery has practical value when it reduces the experimental search burden without obscuring what a prediction represents. Food proteins supply a large sequence space through enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation and digestion, while laboratory characterization must distinguish the activities of intact proteins, mixed hydrolysates, fractions and individual peptides. Computational tools can prioritize candidates within that space, but the biological claim attached to a selected candidate depends on the target used to train or assess the tool. Reviews have described the data–representation–model–validation workflow, its predictive and generative variants, and applications to food-peptide discovery (Chang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Experimental successes give this workflow a substantive basis: BERT-DPPIV guided synthesis and inhibition measurements, machine-learning and virtual-proteolysis strategies identified bovine-milk candidates, and a duckweed study combined peptide profiling and modelling with testing of several activities (Guan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Cournoyer et al., 2025). These studies justify examining how computational screening can support food research.

The negative class is often treated as a single category, but it compresses several kinds of evidence. An experimentally tested peptide that does not meet an activity threshold, a sequence absent from an activity database, an antiviral peptide used as a comparator, and a randomly generated sequence can all be labelled zero in a training file. Their evidential meanings differ. AntiDMPpred, for example, selected negatives from an antiviral resource while stating that their antidiabetic activity had not been reported; ADP-Fuse describes a mixture of random and other-function peptides (Chen et al., 2022; Basith et al., 2023; Qureshi et al., 2014). In general machine learning, learning from positive and unlabelled examples explicitly distinguishes missing labels from confirmed negatives (Elkan and Noto, 2008). At the same time, constructed negatives can be useful: stochastic negative addition improved one molecular-screening benchmark while reducing performance on a temporal benchmark, illustrating that utility depends on the evaluation task (Cáceres et al., 2020). The question is therefore not whether all artificial negatives should be rejected, but what comparison they support and which biological conclusions remain untested.

Existing reviews and benchmarks provide several complementary perspectives, but they do not remove the need to follow a negative label through its use. Chang et al. organize AI-assisted food-peptide screening by workflow and application, whereas Zhao et al. emphasize methodological limitations (Chang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Luo et al. examine whether test conditions and separate functional outputs are actually represented in peptide models; their scope does not include DPP-IV prediction (Luo et al., 2027). Yang et al. distinguish model performance from applicability under food-matrix, task and deployment conditions, and Alaei et al. use structured evidence appraisal to assess operational claims in food processing (Yang et al., 2026; Alaei et al., 2026). More directly, the ADP-Hybrid preprint evaluates homology-aware splits and source-associated patterns in the ADP-Fuse benchmark (Islam et al., 2026, preprint). This review asks a narrower question: how negative-label sources, version changes and evaluation roles constrain what a benchmark score can support in food-peptide screening.

This review asks whether the comparison a model learns, the endpoint it predicts and the way it is evaluated match the intended screening decision. DOME distinguishes provenance, optimization and evaluation; REFORMS addresses validity and reproducibility; and biological benchmarks show that sampling and partitioning warrant examination alongside model architecture (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024; Sidorczuk et al., 2022). This review applies those principles by tracing negative-label sources, membership changes and evaluation roles, then comparing the resulting evidence with the requirements of a food-screening claim. Labels and sampling define what groups a model learns to distinguish; the evaluation procedure defines what its score can support; and an experiment supplies observations and controls for a particular use. These questions are connected, but the word “negative” does not make a training label, an assay non-response and a matched food control interchangeable (Table 1). A benchmark defect can limit what its score supports without invalidating a separate food experiment evaluated on its own design and controls.

# 2. Review scope and evidence approach

This review uses a focused, critical narrative synthesis supported by a bounded provenance audit. Supporting literature was identified from the project's existing food-peptide and modelling library and through citation tracking to primary papers. Sources were retained when they clarified label meaning, negative sampling, evaluation design or the evidence required for a specific screening use. Closely related reviews establish conceptual scope; database publications, methodological studies and original experiments support particular observations. Food examples were selected to compare candidate selection, measurement conditions and material levels, and processing or matrix effects. They are illustrative comparisons, not a systematic sample of applications. The literature and audit cutoff was 2 November 2026; later issue years were retained only for papers already available online, and preprints are identified. Supplementary Table S1 records the sources and extracted claims; no field-wide frequency is inferred from this selection.

The 20 primary audit records, A1–A14 and H1–H6, came from the project's existing DPP-IV/ADP model list. Inclusion and negative-version verification were separate decisions: source-linked records, inaccessible files and tasks without a comparable negative class remained in the audit. Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1) was added as a post-hoc case of re-curation and external testing. The 21-record mapping and task definitions are in Supplementary Table S3; artifact sources and version identifiers are in Supplementary Table S2. General antidiabetic activity, DPP-IV inhibition, diabetes-type classification and residue-level prediction were not treated as interchangeable targets (Basith et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024; Basith et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026, preprint). Non-food and noncomparable records inform the boundaries of resources used in this area rather than demonstrating food-derived inhibition. Where files were obtainable, comparisons concern identified accessible versions, not reconstructed historical runs. Archived recovery for A3, author-repository files for A5, H6's actual input and the pinned B1 implementation retain their distinct source limitations.

Three audit dimensions answer separate questions: negative-version completeness, reference-set relationship and split identity. Within the first dimension, a version was recorded as complete when its full negative membership was obtained, partial when correspondence was incomplete or unresolved, and not verified when the record was source-linked, inaccessible or noncomparable. Figure 1 summarizes the evidence layers and these separate dimensions. These categories describe the negative version only, not label correctness or model reproduction. Membership relationships describe identity, containment, filtering or re-curation relative to a reference set. Split identity records whether published negative partitions equal H1's members, use a study's own split, or are available only as a development pool or partial partition. A6 is retained as source-linked to A2 rather than as an independently verified deposit (Arshad et al., 2024). Available sequence counts, verified complete totals and paper-reported totals are therefore separate. F665 and F966 are reference-set names, not assertions of independent biological lineages. In particular, H6 can be complete for its 629-member input while being a subset of F665, and A9's 899 negative development records are one half of a 1,798-record positive-plus-negative pool, not a verified final fitting subset (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026; Xie et al., 2025).

Files were parsed without changing the originals, retaining raw and normalized sequences with source locators. Normalization comprised uppercase conversion and whitespace removal; only A7's documented terminal `X` padding was removed. The valid dipeptide `NA` was preserved. Record counts and unique identities were retained separately, and comparisons used full-sequence sets rather than similarity or subsequence matching. Opposite-label intersections and train–test intersections across both labels were computed separately from negative-set overlap. H6 splits were reconstructed with stratified `train_test_split`, `test_size=0.2` and seeds 1–10; Supplementary records the software environment. A4 checks executed isolated loader functions and inspected selection calls; Bilingua checks inspected the pinned implementation. No predictors were retrained. Sequence parsing and set intersections were cross-verified programmatically, and known file/text discrepancies were preserved. Detailed scripts, hashes and reconstruction outputs accompany the local supplementary package, whose public-access status is stated explicitly at the end of the manuscript.

# 3. What a negative label means in food-peptide prediction

## 3.1 Activity resources do not define the unmeasured complement

Food-peptide databases organize positive knowledge, but the complement of a collection is not automatically a set of inactive sequences. BIOPEP-UWM links peptides to activities and bibliographic sources; a multifunctional peptide can have more than one activity-associated record. Its structure also distinguishes measured potency information from annotations for which a numerical value is unavailable (Minkiewicz et al., 2019). DFBP similarly separates entries from sequence identities: its publication reports 6,276 entries but 3,997 distinct sequences, with tools for multifunctional analysis and tracing peptides to food proteins (Qin et al., 2022). These distinctions matter when database records become machine-learning samples. Counting every activity entry as a new peptide can duplicate identities, while assigning all sequences absent from one functional category to the negative class can convert incomplete testing into a biological assertion. Neither database membership nor its absence alone resolves whether a peptide was tested for the particular endpoint under review. The source record must be followed back to the measured function and experimental conditions before interpreting a missing annotation as evidence against activity.

More specialized resources help recover context but do not eliminate this inference problem. The Milk Bioactive Peptide Database focuses on experimentally reported milk-protein-derived peptides, BioPepDB integrates food-derived bioactive-peptide information, and FermFooDb links fermented-food sequences to properties such as food source, fermentation conditions and testing models (Nielsen et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Chaudhary et al., 2021). Their different organizing purposes determine which information is readily recoverable. A fermentation record can help identify the production context of an observed sequence; a milk-peptide record can connect it to a precursor and an activity study. Those fields do not establish that all unlisted peptide–endpoint combinations were assayed and found inactive. Consequently, the absence of an experimentally verified inactive library does not force one inevitable modelling strategy, nor does it justify treating every unrecorded combination as a true negative. It identifies a gap in evidence that can be handled through a declared sampling convention, an unlabelled-data approach, additional testing or a more limited prediction claim. The choice should follow the intended screening task rather than the convenience of a database export.

The source of a sequence also needs to be separated from evidence about its function. BioDADPep brings together peptides associated with type 1 and type 2 diabetes and their targets; UniProt provides protein sequences and annotations that can support precursor assignment and sequence retrieval (Roy and Teron, 2019; The UniProt Consortium, 2025). These resources are not interchangeable labels for food-derived inhibition. The reviewed two-layer ADP-Fuse model first distinguishes ADPs from non-ADPs and then separates diabetes-type labels, whereas the T1DM/T2DM study uses those disease categories directly (Basith et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024). A zero in the second task may therefore represent a peptide associated with one diabetes type rather than a peptide without antidiabetic function. Protein-source inference has a further limitation: finding a peptide as a substring of a food protein establishes sequence compatibility with a precursor, not that a particular process releases it at a relevant concentration. These distinctions should be retained when building a food-peptide dataset, otherwise source identity, disease association and measured inhibition can become different meanings of the same apparently simple binary target.

## 3.2 Other-function, random and null comparators answer different questions

Other-function peptides are attractive comparators because they are available as experimentally studied sequences, but experimental confirmation of one function does not exclude another. AVPdb records antiviral peptides and DRAMP records antimicrobial peptides, whereas SATPdb and THPdb organize broader therapeutic sequences or approved peptide and protein products (Qureshi et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2016; Usmani et al., 2017; Kang et al., 2019). Their existence supports the identity and annotated function of an imported record, not its inactivity against DPP-IV or another food-relevant enzyme. AntiDMPpred makes this distinction visible by drawing negatives from AVPdb while describing antidiabetic activity as unreported (Chen et al., 2022). In a broader functional benchmark, Bizzotto et al. construct one-versus-rest tasks using the remaining functional classes as negatives (Bizzotto et al., 2024). That design is meaningful for classification under the adopted label scheme, but it does not turn all members of the other classes into experimentally demonstrated non-inhibitors. The interpretation depends on whether the task is to recover an annotation category, distinguish sampled groups, or predict a previously unmeasured biological response.

Multifunctional findings show why this qualification is substantive rather than merely terminological. In the duckweed study by Cournoyer et al., tested peptides included sequences with activity across DPP-IV inhibition, ACE inhibition and antioxidant assays (Cournoyer et al., 2025). DFBP also explicitly accommodates multiple activities associated with the same sequence (Qin et al., 2022). These examples are sufficient to reject the universal inference that an annotation in one category makes a peptide inactive in another. The defensible interpretation is conditional: such a comparator can help distinguish the sampled functional groups, while the target activity of each untested member remains unresolved. Positive–unlabelled learning formalizes a related distinction between the observation that a label is missing and the underlying class itself (Elkan and Noto, 2008). Its assumptions, including how labelled positives were selected, would still have to be evaluated before applying that framework to a particular food-peptide resource.

Randomly sampled and generated negatives raise a different issue: they can be useful modelling controls without being observations of biological inactivity. H1 describes random unique peptides together with functional non-inhibitory wording, while A2 reports random and other-function components (Charoenkwan et al., 2020; Basith et al., 2023). Outside peptide inhibition, Cáceres et al. treat untested molecule–target pairs as transient stochastic negatives during training. Their approach improved performance on a drug-screening benchmark but produced a modest decline on a temporal benchmark, demonstrating a context-dependent benefit rather than a universal superiority of random negatives (Cáceres et al., 2020). In antimicrobial prediction, Sidorczuk et al. explicitly vary negative-sampling procedures and find that benchmarking depends on the relationship between training and evaluation negatives (Sidorczuk et al., 2022). These studies provide an empirical basis for examining sampling choices. The review therefore distinguishes the operational usefulness of constructed negatives from a biological claim about every sampled sequence.

The terminology used for generated controls should reflect the same distinction. A12's accessible spreadsheet identifies 200 synthetic null peptides, but those records could not be mapped to the larger training and evaluation partitions reported in the paper (Rahmani Asl et al., 2026). “Null” describes the role assigned to those sequences, not evidence that a specified DPP-IV assay was performed on each one. Similarly, a de novo generator may produce candidates outside the training sequence set without establishing that they are inactive, safe, digestively stable or attainable in a food process. For a constructed comparator to support a screening claim, its sampling domain and evaluation role must therefore be stated: sequence length, residue composition, source restrictions and the way it is separated from positives can change the contrast presented to the classifier. Whether those differences are appropriate depends on the candidate population to which the model will be applied. A declared artificial control is scientifically interpretable; a construction label silently promoted into an experimentally confirmed negative is not.

## 3.3 Experimental inactivity is endpoint- and condition-specific

An experimental non-response can define a negative only in relation to a specified endpoint, concentration range, decision criterion, assay sensitivity and controls. It does not establish that the sequence lacks activity under all conditions. The milk-peptide QSAR study illustrates the importance of retaining assay context when combining measured potency values (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016); the distinction between ranking and numerical prediction is examined in Section 5.2. Material identity can also change during testing. Substrate-type, prodrug-type and true-inhibitor behaviour were distinguished after incubation with DPP-IV, showing that the response may concern a parent peptide, its products or a mixture (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2014). Section 5.3 considers the processing implications. These findings explain why endpoint, material and conditions belong with an activity label; they do not identify the biological cause of the conflicting records examined below. A sequence-only binary dataset cannot represent a condition-dependent difference if the conditions and decision rule have been discarded.

These distinctions support the evidence categories summarized in Table 1 without requiring a new score. A database-unannotated record carries missing endpoint evidence; an other-function record carries positive evidence for a different endpoint; a random or generated record carries a construction rule; and an experimentally non-responsive record carries a result under specified conditions. More than one description may apply to the same sequence. Source descriptions, construction rules, annotation status and experimental verification should therefore be recorded as nonexclusive attributes rather than ordered grades of biological truth. The literature does not justify ranking every artificial comparator below every measured result irrespective of task, because a measured result may itself concern a different endpoint or unsuitable conditions. It does justify requiring the intended contrast to be explicit. The next section considers how selection from these evidence categories, and subsequent benchmark inheritance, determines what the model evaluation actually tests.

# 4. How construction, reuse and evaluation define the tested comparison

## 4.1 The sampled contrast can differ from the intended biological task

A negative-sampling procedure determines more than a class balance. If positives and negatives differ systematically in source, length, residue composition or sequence families, a classifier can exploit those differences without resolving the biological distinction intended by the user. Shortcut-learning theory describes rules that work within a benchmark but fail under changed conditions, while systematic biological audits show the value of controls that isolate suspected sources of signal (Geirhos et al., 2020; Eid et al., 2021). The relevant conclusion is not that a model using such features must be wrong: peptide properties can be related to real activity. The concern is that a benchmark may not separate task-relevant information from collection-specific patterns. Sidorczuk et al.'s negative-sampling experiments in antimicrobial prediction demonstrate this dependence empirically (Sidorczuk et al., 2022). Their findings support testing the comparator distribution, but their numerical effects should not be imported into DPP-IV or general ADP prediction without a matched experiment. Distributional concerns are hypotheses about a particular evaluation until the corresponding controls are performed.

The ADP-Hybrid preprint provides a directly related, but separate, analysis of this issue. Using the ADP-Fuse benchmark, Islam et al. examine source attribution, sequence similarity, length baselines and homology-aware evaluation (Islam et al., 2026, preprint). Within the subset of 218 of 877 positive peptides assigned to candidate precursors, they report strong correspondence between diabetes-type labels and source proteins, including 140 human-insulin fragments with the type-1 label and 76 bovine-milk-protein fragments with the type-2 label. They also report changes in model comparisons when homologous sequences are separated. These observations strengthen the need to distinguish source-associated prediction from the target function, especially in resources combining endogenous and food-related sequences. They do not establish the same relationship for unassigned peptides, and the reported architecture and split effects belong to that preprint's reconstruction, not to the present audit. Its limitations—including incomplete precursor attribution and differences between reconstruction and original execution—must accompany its use as evidence.

Comparisons with simple features can help determine which information a sampled task rewards. Bizzotto et al. compare encodings and classifiers across nine functional groups with different sample sizes and sequence characteristics, while Adamczyk et al. report strong performance from local molecular fingerprints across multiple peptide datasets (Bizzotto et al., 2024; Adamczyk et al., 2026). These findings motivate informative baselines rather than an algorithm ranking for food-derived antidiabetic peptides. If a proposed model improves on a length or composition baseline, the remaining question is whether that improvement persists when the relevant source or sequence dependence is addressed. Conversely, a strong simple baseline can reveal that a collection is readily separable without showing which feature causes biological activity. Combined with direct negative-sampling experiments, these studies support examining the comparator distribution and the evaluation population before attributing benchmark gains to a more biologically informative representation (Sidorczuk et al., 2022).

## 4.2 What exact benchmark reuse adds—and does not add

The bounded audit makes several inheritance relationships explicit (Figure 2). Of the 21 study records, 17 had retrievable negative-sequence versions and 14 were complete for the specified version; the corresponding primary-set counts were 16/20 and 13/20. These are coverage counts within the selected corpus, not estimates of field-wide reproducibility; the status totals are 14 complete, three partial or unresolved, and four source-linked, unavailable or noncomparable records (A6, A8, A10 and A11; Supplementary Table S3). Seven audited versions—iDPPIV-SCM (H1), iDPPIV-SI (H2), iDPPIV-PC (H3), IPPF-FE (H5), StackDPPIV (A3), StructuralDPPIV (A4) and BERT-DPPIV (A5)—contained the same 665 negative identities and the same 532/133 published training/test members (Charoenkwan et al., 2020; Zou, 2024; Zou and Yin, 2021; Yu and Luo, 2023; Charoenkwan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2023). The A3 comparison uses archived recovery and the A5 comparison author-repository files. Exact member identity, rather than matching counts alone, supports the finding. Several original papers explicitly disclose reuse, consistent with using a common benchmark for algorithm comparison. The seven studies therefore analyse one shared set of negative identities. This supports controlled comparison on that reference, while the number of papers does not count newly collected negative populations.

Related versions require more precise descriptions than either “same data” or “new data.” SeqDPPIV (H6) uses an input containing 664 positive and 629 negative records; its negatives are exactly the F665 sequences no longer than 33 residues, and its notebook uses its own random split (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026). It is complete for that 629-member input, not a partially recovered 665-member benchmark. BBATProt (H4) supplies a 532-negative training file identical to the H1/H3 training members, but the reported 133 test members were not obtained from the reviewed release (Wang et al., 2025). The test file of iDPPIV-PC (H3) remains only a candidate source link, and the likely reference-number error in BBATProt (H4) does not establish its actual test members. These distinctions avoid two opposite mistakes: treating a filtered but complete dataset as missing data, and treating a plausible upstream link as direct recovery of an unavailable partition. The complete, partial and reference-relation fields in Table 2 are deliberately separate for this reason.

The deep learning study by Yue et al. (A7) shows why normalization and release correspondence belong in the same discussion. Its repository supplies 4,200 training and 1,050 test negatives, whereas the paper describes a training construction that retains 3,970 of an initial 4,000 negatives (Yue et al., 2024). The correspondence between those memberships remains unresolved. The README also specifies terminal `X` padding; 4,189 of the 4,200 training negatives carry it. Removing only the documented padding yields intersections of 51 with F665, 101 with F966, 120 with the complete ADP-Fuse (A2) negative set and 205 with AntiDMPpred (A1) (Figure 3). Those numbers describe the normalized repository version, not a recovered final paper-level training set. They show that apparently small raw-string overlap can be a representation artifact, and that separately collected resources need not be sequence-disjoint. By contrast, the absence of an exact intersection after valid normalization still says nothing about near-homology, common precursor fragments or shared assay origins (Whalen et al., 2022). Different overlap questions must therefore retain their own definitions and limits.

## 4.3 Inherited labels and changes introduced by re-curation

The seven fixed-partition F665 versions also retain seven opposite-label identities. Four—GPFPIIV, LHLPLP, LPVPQ and LTQTPVVVPPF—occur in both training classes. Three cross the deposited boundary with opposite labels: FAQTQS is a training negative and test positive, whereas LTDVEN and VRGPFP are training positives and test negatives. Their exact source positions are listed in Table 3 and the supplementary occurrence records. Enumerating these identities across seven versions produces 49 sequence-by-version records, not 49 independent errors. The configuration is a concrete example of how reuse can carry a label problem that aggregate sample counts conceal. It is stronger evidence than a general suspicion about dataset quality, but narrower than an explanation of performance: a sequence-only binary target is inconsistent across these records, while the underlying assay histories and the effect on a fitted model have not been adjudicated.

Filtering and re-curation produce different outcomes for the same conflict set. The 629-member negative input of SeqDPPIV (H6) retains all seven opposite-label identities, but their placement changes under its own partitioning. In the audit's specified ten reconstructions, nine seeds place between one and four conflicting identities across the boundary, while one seed places none; the input conflicts remain irrespective of placement (Balachandran and Bhadra, 2026). Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1), in contrast, excludes all seven identities from both classes in its accessible training, internal-test and external files (Zhang et al., 2026). Its benchmark negatives comprise 658 retained F665 identities plus three additional sequences, and a separate external panel adds five negatives. Thus, the seven missing F665 negatives are exactly the conflict identities. This is consistent with the paper's stated re-curation and explicit discussion of earlier label conflicts.

The literature and audit together distinguish membership correction from biological validation. Excluding contradictory records can remove an ambiguity in a sequence-only target, but it does not verify all remaining negatives as inactive. Keeping both labels may be defensible only if the differing endpoints, peptide forms or conditions are represented so that the records do not purport to describe the same prediction target. DOME asks for source, partition and redundancy information, while database publications describe structures for linking activity entries to original reports (Walsh et al., 2021; Minkiewicz et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2022). Those principles provide a basis for version-aware re-curation rather than silently editing a sequence list. The present findings show retained or excluded conflicts in specific files. Removing difficult or contradictory examples may also change the sampled task, so any revised benchmark should state which members and labels changed and what comparison is still intended.

## 4.4 Source reuse across development and evaluation roles

The ADP-Fuse–BertADP–FedAntiDiaPep lineage (A2–A9–A13) adds a role dimension to inheritance. ADP-Fuse (A2) and the DQN ensemble (A14) share a released 1,399-negative Layer-1 set with identical 658/741 training/test members (Basith et al., 2023; Kumar and Singh, 2026). The complete 966-negative set of BertADP (A9) consists of the 658 A2 training negatives plus 308 A2 test negatives. Of the latter, 241 enter A9's negative development component and 67 its external test component; 433 A2 test negatives do not appear in A9. The full A9 development pool contains 899 negatives and 899 positives, or 1,798 records, before a paper-reported internal 80/20 division whose final membership was not recovered (Xie et al., 2025). The published 899-negative training and 67-negative test files of FedAntiDiaPep (A13) match the A9 development and external members (Shoyshob et al., 2026). This establishes a chain of membership and role assignments, but not identical internal fitting subsets or historical execution across the models.

BertADP (A9) explicitly discloses drawing additional negatives from the earlier test pool and selecting the external negatives from the remainder (Xie et al., 2025). Its own development and external sets have no exact sequence intersection, including when labels are combined. The earlier test origin therefore should not be described as proof that A9 trained on its own external panel. Equally, the word “external” does not make those 67 negative identities new relative to A2. This distinction follows the broader separation between source reuse, biological dependence and evaluation-information use in machine-learning methodology (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023; Walsh et al., 2021). The source history is relevant to interpreting evidence accumulation; a claim of adaptive overfitting or score inflation would require additional evidence about how previous results affected decisions. Table 2 retains the exact role assignments, while Figure 3 shows the corresponding set-level intersections. These membership comparisons leave a further question: whether outcomes from an evaluation set influenced the decisions used to construct or select the evaluated model.

## 4.5 From sample separation to decision separation

DOME and REFORMS treat evaluation as part of an entire analytical procedure rather than a label attached to a final file (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024). Preprocessing, feature selection, tuning and the choice between candidate methods can use outcome information even if the final model is not explicitly fitted on every evaluation sample. Cawley and Talbot show why model selection itself can overfit an evaluation criterion and must be considered in performance assessment (Cawley and Talbot, 2010). This principle helps interpret a test set's role without confusing it with sequence provenance. A shared benchmark may be kept separate from fitting in a particular analysis, while a newly collected dataset may cease to be untouched after repeated decisions are made against it. Leakage reviews provide a broader taxonomy (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). The necessary evidence is the actual data flow and selection rule for the procedure being examined.

The saved StructuralDPPIV (A4) implementation provides one directly inspectable example. Its DPP-IV loader changes `valid` to `test`, and isolated reader calls return the same 266 ordered records—133 positive and 133 negative—for both roles. The training entry point selects the best checkpoint using validation accuracy before calling the test evaluation on that checkpoint (Wang et al., 2024). This establishes a use of the same file in selection and testing within the inspected code path. It does not reconstruct the historical run that produced each published score; the source is a locally retained author-code version, and no run log was available. The paper reports 113 test examples per class, whereas the inspected files contain 133 per class, further limiting identification of the released implementation with the reported experiment. The distinction is consequential: the source supports a precise statement about implementation logic, not a claim that all reported scores are inflated.

Bilingua-DPP-IV (B1) illustrates several levels of test-information use in one saved implementation. The stacking branch fits a meta-model using `test_labels`, the optimized-threshold branch maximizes test MCC, and the ensemble-comparison block selects `best_method` from test MCC values before reporting the selected method's metrics (Zhang et al., 2026). The paper also describes comparing the ensemble strategies on the internal test data before selecting the average model. These observations distinguish fitting parameters, choosing a threshold and choosing a method. The final `avg` strategy is not the stacking fit, but that does not remove the observed use of test outcomes in selecting among strategies. Its separate 49-record external panel, containing 44 positives and five negatives, must be evaluated on its own boundary: the audit found no exact overlap with Bilingua's internal data, and the inspected internal-test operations do not prove that external labels were used in the same way. The five negatives also limit the breadth of negative examples represented by that panel.

# 5. What validation permits a food-peptide screening claim

A food-screening claim can involve a different population, endpoint or comparator from the benchmark used to develop a predictor. Candidate selection determines which molecules are tested; assay and material choices determine the response measured; and processing or matrix comparisons determine the incremental effect relevant to a food use. The following sections compare evidence addressing these three changes. They do not require every study to complete a universal sequence of validation stages: each observation must answer the question posed by the intended use.

## 5.1 Candidate confirmation is not automatically validation of a classifier

Candidate-testing studies can provide important evidence even when they do not estimate whole-model classification performance. BERT-DPPIV reports experimental work on selected tripeptides and pentapeptides, while machine-learning and virtual-proteolysis studies identify bovine-milk and rice-protein candidates and test their inhibition (Guan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025). These experiments support the activities of the tested materials under their respective conditions. The further question—how accurately a tool separates active and inactive candidates in a screening population—depends on how candidates were chosen and which prediction groups were tested. Testing only a highly selected positive shortlist does not observe false negatives among discarded candidates, and combining classifier scores with docking and manual filters makes the yield a property of the pipeline rather than necessarily of the classifier alone. It follows from the difference between identifying useful hits and estimating error rates for a declared candidate population (Walsh et al., 2021; Cawley and Talbot, 2010).

The comparator's function therefore needs to be explicit in the experiment as well as in the training data. A reference inhibitor can demonstrate that an enzyme assay responds as expected, whereas a sampled low-score peptide tests a different proposition about prediction; a substrate-free blank addresses yet another measurement component. These controls cannot be substituted for each other simply because they are all described as negative or positive controls. The rice-peptide screening paper, for example, combines a machine-learning stage, docking and molecular simulation before testing a small selected set (Cheng et al., 2025). Goat-milk studies likewise combine virtual digestion, ranking and synthetic-peptide assays, with different levels of evidence for the candidates' occurrence in hydrolysates (M. Liu et al., 2026; Dang et al., 2025). Such designs are informative when described as candidate-discovery pipelines. Claims about enrichment, specificity or false-negative risk require a sampling and comparison design that directly addresses those quantities, rather than treating the number of confirmed shortlisted peptides as a general classifier accuracy.

The experimental studies supply different combinations of discovery and use evidence. Duckweed hydrolysate profiling was linked to modelling and synthetic-peptide assays, including activity at more than one endpoint (Cournoyer et al., 2025). Oyster and pumpkin-seed studies likewise tested computationally prioritized candidates, whereas the red-alga study additionally quantified a specific peptide in digestion products (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026; Kumagai et al., 2026). Candidate assays establish responses of the selected molecules; profiling or targeted quantification addresses their occurrence in the source material; separate functional assays address additional endpoints. These observations can complement one another within a discovery pipeline, but none substitutes for sampling the prediction groups needed to evaluate classification errors. The relevant comparison is therefore between the questions actually tested, rather than the number of computational steps or food sources represented. Evidence that a useful candidate was found remains valuable even when the broader performance of the selection rule is unresolved.

## 5.2 Endpoint, material and assay context change the meaning of a comparison

The distinction between ranking and quantitative prediction is particularly clear in the milk-peptide QSAR study. Nongonierma and FitzGerald selected and synthesized peptides from a set reported in the human intestinal tract, then compared predicted and measured DPP-IV inhibition (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016). The ranking of competitive inhibitors was broadly conserved, but absolute predicted IC50 values did not agree with the experimental values; one descriptor model tended to underestimate and the other to overestimate. A model can therefore be useful for prioritization while not supporting a concentration-level claim. The same paper discusses difficulties in combining potency measurements obtained under different assay conditions. For a binary model, converting these measurements to a thresholded label does not make the underlying incompatibility disappear. A defensible negative comparison must identify the endpoint and decision threshold represented, especially when transferring a classifier trained on heterogeneous literature labels into an experiment with a specific enzyme preparation and protocol.

Within-study comparisons are especially valuable because they reduce the temptation to compare unrelated numerical values as though they were measured on one scale. In chickpea protein hydrolysates, Zan et al. report IAIPPGIPYW at an enzyme-assay IC50 of 12.43 ± 0.48 μM, while its Caco-2-cell result is 138.63 ± 14.98 μM; PPGIPYW similarly has different enzyme and cellular potency values (Zan et al., 2023). The difference is not evidence that one assay is erroneous. The systems measure inhibition under different conditions and biological organization. Goat-milk peptides GPFPLL and LPYPY were also examined through purified-peptide inhibition, digestion-related testing and a cellular experiment, with endpoints and concentrations that should not be collapsed into one potency label (Dang et al., 2025). These examples show why confirmation at a later experimental layer must be interpreted as a new observation, not as a numerical repetition of the earlier one. Classification labels built from one layer should retain that provenance.

Material identity is equally important. A mass-based IC50 for a protein hydrolysate measures a mixture, whereas a molar IC50 for a synthesized sequence concerns a defined molecular species. The black-bean study reports inhibition by hydrolysate fractions alongside computational analyses of candidate sequences; the fraction-level inhibition cannot be assigned to a single docked peptide without a direct attribution experiment (Ramirez-Lozano et al., 2025). In probiotic-fermented milk, comparisons across milk sources and fermentation conditions similarly concern products containing multiple peptides and other constituents (Mudgil et al., 2024). Resource-level labels and table headings should preserve whether the evidence unit is a sequence, fraction, hydrolysate or food matrix. A sequence classifier cannot receive validation from a mixture's response solely because the predicted peptide may be present in that mixture. Conversely, a mixture-level finding can be valuable for functional-ingredient development without establishing one constituent as its causal agent. The appropriate negative or matched control follows the material and the claim.

A direct comparison of whey protein isolate and hydrolysate adds an important qualification to the assumption that more hydrolysis necessarily means stronger performance on every endpoint. Tenenbaum et al. applied simulated gastrointestinal digestion and examined human recombinant DPP-IV, a cellular DPP-IV system, GLP-1 secretion and intestinal-barrier passage (Tenenbaum et al., 2023). The two digested materials showed similar DPP-IV inhibition in the reported direct comparisons, while the prehydrolysed material differed in peptide distribution and stimulated greater GLP-1 secretion under the tested conditions. The value of this result is not a universal superiority or equivalence claim for hydrolysates. It is the demonstration that one processing change can have different consequences for different measured responses. A single label such as antidiabetic would hide that distinction, and a negative result for one endpoint would not make the material negative for all glucose-homeostasis-related mechanisms. Such matched comparisons are especially useful for defining the target that an AI model should predict.

## 5.3 Processing, digestion and food matrices require their own evidence

Predicted release, detected release and retained activity are distinguishable steps. A virtual proteolysis result indicates that a sequence is compatible with an assumed cleavage procedure; it does not measure its yield or survival under the actual food process. This limitation is addressed more directly when candidate identity is traced into an experimental digest. In the red-alga study, EVYR was identified as a DPP-IV inhibitor and quantified by targeted analysis in pepsin–trypsin digestion products of both dulse powder and extracted protein (Kumagai et al., 2026). The two preparations yielded different measured peptide contents, demonstrating that precursor compatibility alone does not specify the amount present in a particular material. The integrated rice-bran study also combines peptide identification, candidate synthesis, cell-based inhibition and digestion-stability observations (Zeng et al., 2026). These designs add evidence beyond sequence classification, but their results still do not establish systemic exposure or a human glycaemic effect for the identified peptide. Each additional observation should retain its analytical endpoint.

Digestion can change both the candidate and the reference against which it should be evaluated. The DPP-IV hydrolysis study distinguishes peptides whose activity decreases after cleavage from prodrug-type material that yields more active products (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2014). This argues against using “digestion resistance” as the only desirable outcome: a parent sequence may disappear while a relevant activity remains or changes. Reviews of biological-barrier prediction similarly distinguish properties of the peptide from the evidence required for passage through a barrier (An et al., 2026). For the present topic, the implication is to avoid labelling a peptide as universally useful or ineffective from an isolated stability or inhibition result. The measured digest, its identified products and any activity attributed to them need to be connected. Neither persistence of the parent sequence nor an inhibition percentage for the whole digest alone establishes all of those links, and an appropriate food comparison should state which one is being tested.

The distinction between virtual and observed processing is also supported by an experiment devoted to proteolytic stability rather than a new inhibition assay. Turalić et al. compared predicted cleavage sites with in vitro digestion of five known milk-derived DPP-IV inhibitory peptides and an additional cleavage-site validation dataset (Turalić et al., 2025). The tested peptides were largely stable under the specified three-hour incubations, but several cleavage predictions were not experimentally confirmed. This study does not newly establish the peptides' DPP-IV potency, and it should not be counted as an independent activity-confirmation experiment. It provides a separate validation of the processing prediction used to reason about those peptides. The example shows why a negative cleavage prediction, a stable parent sequence and an inhibitory response are not interchangeable observations: each belongs to a different model or experimental endpoint and must be assessed against its own reference.

The oat-peptide biscuit study shows how a food matrix supplies a necessary comparator rather than merely a delivery vehicle. Yao et al. incorporated linear LQAFEPLR and a cyclic variant into biscuits, assessed heating and simulated digestion, and measured the resulting enzyme inhibition and starch-digestion behaviour (Yao et al., 2026). The control biscuit digesta already produced 29.2% α-glucosidase inhibition. The reported values for fortified biscuit digesta were 39.3% for the linear peptide and 46.9% for the cyclic form; the cyclic comparison, but not the linear increase, was statistically significant relative to control for that endpoint. Reporting only the two fortified percentages would obscure the background response and overstate what the experiment established. This is directly relevant to negative evidence: the comparator in a food-use experiment may be a matched processed matrix, not a collection of unrelated peptide sequences. A model trained against random or other-function peptides does not by itself establish the additional benefit of an ingredient in that matrix.

These comparisons address different sources of uncertainty in a screening claim. Selective synthesis leaves outcomes for untested candidates unobserved, limiting estimates of missed actives or enrichment. A change from an enzyme assay to cells, or from a pure peptide to a hydrolysate, changes the measured target or attribution unit rather than merely adding another replicate. Processing studies distinguish predicted release from measured occurrence, while a matched food matrix reveals whether the tested addition improves the specified endpoint above its background response. Thus, the QSAR, cellular, digestion and biscuit results cannot be pooled into one measure of successful validation. They identify which observation is needed for a particular decision and which already available evidence remains informative (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016; Zan et al., 2023; Kumagai et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2026). A study can supply strong evidence for one of these judgments while leaving the others unresolved.

## 5.4 Safety and multifunctionality cannot be inferred from activity ranking

Safety screening deserves the same distinction between prediction and measurement as functional activity. The TIFS review of food-peptide toxicity describes AI as a means of preliminary risk screening and prioritization while retaining the role of chemical, cellular and animal assays (X. Liu et al., 2026). The NoiTox preprint further examines label noise in peptide-toxicity benchmarks (Almeida et al., 2026, preprint), although its specific findings cannot be generalized automatically to antidiabetic labels. A negative toxicity prediction is therefore not experimental evidence of safety for every concentration, digestion product or formulation. Likewise, activity at multiple endpoints should be supported endpoint by endpoint rather than inferred from database co-annotation or docking against several proteins (Luo et al., 2027). The same rule should apply consistently: a label or score supports the endpoint and conditions represented by its evidence, and claims about additional properties need additional observations.

# 6. Interpreting negative evidence for a defined screening use

## 6.1 Align the comparator with the claim rather than with a convenient label

The same predictor may be used to compare algorithms, select candidates, estimate potency or support a food formulation, but these uses require different observations. Table 4 links each intended use to the comparison it needs and the inference that remains outside its scope. It does not certify the reviewed models for those uses. Within-benchmark evaluation can establish discrimination against assigned labels, candidate assays can establish activity of tested molecules, and matched food experiments can establish an incremental response under their conditions. None automatically supplies the missing evidence for the others. DOME and REFORMS provide principles for assessing the data and decisions underlying a performance claim, while FAIR supports access and reuse of the research objects needed to inspect that claim (Walsh et al., 2021; Kapoor et al., 2024; Wilkinson et al., 2016). Accessibility makes verification possible; it does not itself establish biological label validity or application performance.

## 6.2 Priorities that follow from the evidence

For dataset assembly, an assigned zero should retain the evidence that produced it. If the designation comes from absent annotation, another recorded function or a generation rule, that origin should remain explicit when the record is exported or reused. If it comes from a measured non-response, the endpoint, material, conditions and decision criterion should accompany it. Existing resources retain parts of this context, including source proteins, functional records and fermentation information (Minkiewicz et al., 2019; Nielsen et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Chaudhary et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2022). The practical requirement is to preserve those distinctions through benchmark construction. When curation changes members or labels, the revised target set should be identified before comparing scores with earlier versions. The F665 and Bilingua cases show why membership changes are inspectable and consequential to the comparison, even though removing contradictory records alone does not verify the remaining negatives biologically.

For model evaluation, the required separation follows the intended claim. Comparing algorithms on a fixed benchmark requires a stated target, evaluation members and metric, together with a selection procedure that does not adapt to the reported test outcomes. Generalization to different sequence families or sources requires a design that addresses those dependencies; exact duplicate removal alone cannot establish it (Whalen et al., 2022; Walsh et al., 2021). The ADP-Hybrid reconstruction and fingerprint benchmarks motivate homology-aware comparisons and simple-feature baselines, but their numerical results remain specific to those studies (Islam et al., 2026, preprint; Adamczyk et al., 2026). If checkpoint, threshold or ensemble choice uses test labels, those choices are part of the procedure whose performance needs separate assessment (Cawley and Talbot, 2010). Accordingly, the A4 and B1 code observations concern the inspected selection operations; neither newly named files nor public availability establish that a historical evaluation was untouched.

For candidate screening, the next comparison should address the uncertainty limiting the intended decision. A ranking claim requires measured ordering across the sampled candidates; confirming activity in a few high-ranked molecules does not test that ordering or enrichment over an alternative selection. A numerical potency claim additionally requires comparison of predictions with measured concentration–response estimates and their errors under defined conditions (Nongonierma and FitzGerald, 2016; Zan et al., 2023). If the claim is release during a food process, identification and quantification in the relevant digest address an uncertainty that synthetic-peptide inhibition cannot resolve (Kumagai et al., 2026). If the claim is an added food effect, the matched processed matrix supplies the necessary comparison (Yao et al., 2026). These are distinct uses of computational, chemical and biological evidence, not a mandatory progression to every experimental level (Liang et al., 2025). Safety and additional functional claims likewise need observations appropriate to their endpoint and exposure conditions (X. Liu et al., 2026).

## 6.3 Scope of the synthesis and unresolved work

The synthesis combines a purposeful literature selection with a bounded audit, so neither source yields a prevalence estimate for the field. The audit verifies particular memberships and code paths; it does not quantify the performance effects of those findings. BBATProt (H4) test members, the paper-to-repository mapping in Yue et al. (A7), and the complete corpus of the de novo framework (A12) remain unresolved. The AntiT2DMP-Pred (A10) file-access failure is a dated observation, not evidence that data were never released. Source-level adjudication of the seven conflicting labels, systematic homology analysis and historical execution remain outside the completed work. Complete negative membership also leaves other data questions open: the retained StructuralDPPIV files contain 531 positive training records, and the Bilingua positive benchmark contains 839 records representing 838 identities (Supplementary Table S6). Completeness therefore describes a specified negative version rather than certifying all labels, files or model results.

The most useful next comparisons would separate the effects currently combined under broad claims of improved prediction. A matched modelling study could vary the negative construction while holding the positive set and selection procedure constant; an experimental screening study could test defined portions of both prioritized and deprioritized candidates; and a food study could compare the same candidate in solution, after processing and in a matched matrix. Each design addresses a different uncertainty identified by the literature. The present audit supplies retrievable examples of inherited members, contradictory labels and evaluation roles, while the food experiments show why a response measured at one level cannot simply stand for the next. Progress therefore depends on connecting these observations through an explicitly stated screening objective. More elaborate architectures or larger collections can contribute, but neither resolves a mismatch between the comparison represented by the data and the claim expected from the model.

# 7. Conclusions

Negative evidence in antidiabetic peptide prediction is heterogeneous: missing annotation, other-function activity, stochastic construction and measured non-response support different comparisons. Database and experimental literature show why the endpoint, tested conditions, material identity and sampling domain must remain connected to the label. The bounded audit demonstrates how exact members and opposite-label conflicts can persist through benchmark reuse, and how re-curation can change those members; a separate lineage shows disclosed reassignment of earlier test negatives to later development and external roles. Implementation cases further distinguish sequence separation from the use of test information in selection. For food-peptide discovery, these findings support a practical requirement: interpret each prediction and validation result against the comparator and conditions actually represented, and design the next experiment to address the claim that remains untested. Shared benchmarks can remain useful, constructed negatives can serve declared modelling purposes, and additional experiments can add meaningful evidence. None of these observations alone establishes performance inflation, universal biological inactivity or effectiveness in a food application. The appropriate advance is a more traceable connection between the negative example, the evaluated task and the intended screening decision.
